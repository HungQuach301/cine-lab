In 1954, a computer translated more than sixty Russian sentences into English. The forecasts said that within five years, machines could translate almost anything. But it took far longer. And when the machines finally arrived, what happened to the people who carry words from one language to another?

[01]
At dusk, the lamplighter passes a library. Inside, volunteers bend over messages written in Dutch, Polish, German, and French. The lamplighter raises the flame to the post by the window, and inside, a reading lamp comes on. Lamplighting is a job machines took over long ago. Tonight, it opens the story of another.

[02]
That photograph was taken in 1942, at the Red Cross in Washington. Translation was done by people, one sentence at a time. Interpreting, for speech, worked the same way. The speaker stopped, and then the interpreter said it again in another language. This is called consecutive interpreting. A few years later, a trial changed how interpreters worked.

[03]
In October 1945, Leon Dostert, who had been General Eisenhower's interpreter during the war, was asked to set up the interpretation for the war crimes tribunal at Nuremberg. He chose a method many thought would not work: simultaneous interpretation, speaking while the speaker is still speaking. IBM donated the equipment. Every participant in the courtroom wore headphones and could switch between four language channels. The interpreters sat in booths, each working into their own language only, with a warning light to ask a speaker to slow down. Behind the booths, translators worked through thousands of documents.

[04]
It was the first large-scale use of simultaneous interpretation, and it worked. The new equipment did not replace the interpreters. It changed their job, from waiting for the speaker to stop, to speaking at the same time. In 1946, Dostert installed a similar system at the United Nations. In 1949, he moved to Georgetown University.

[05]
The lamplighter walks on. After the war, Washington worried about how little it knew of Soviet work, published in Russian. Dostert, who had used machines to help interpreters, now tried a machine that would translate on its own. On January 7, 1954, at IBM's headquarters in New York, his Georgetown team and IBM showed a computer translating Russian sentences. It knew two hundred fifty words and six rules of grammar. The next morning it was on front pages. IBM's head of applied science said a machine built just for translation could be ready within three to five years.

[06]
Twelve years later, the National Academy of Sciences took stock. Its committee, called ALPAC, first counted the people. In October 1962, the federal government employed two hundred sixty-two translators and clerk-translators in the United States. A government research service kept four thousand translators under contract, but in an average month it used only about three hundred of them. The committee found no shortage of translators. Then it counted the money. Over ten years, the government had spent some twenty million dollars on machine translation and related research.

[07]
The results were thin. When the Georgetown project tried to produce useful output in 1962, after eight years of work, it had to have people correct the machine's output. The committee called this postediting. The postedited translation took slightly longer, and cost more, than ordinary human translation. Its conclusion was blunt: there was no immediate or predictable prospect of useful machine translation.

[08]
But the committee also found a machine that did help. At a military translation agency in Germany, a computer produced a glossary of the technical terms in each text. Translators with the glossary finished much faster, and made a third fewer errors. So the committee recommended something different: research into language, and machine aids for human translators. In 1966, the useful machine was the one that helped the person at the desk.

[09]
The lamplighter walks on. The library goes dark. Across the street, in an apartment, a screen is still lit. Someone pastes a paragraph into a box, and a moment later it comes back in another language.

[10]
After 2010, Google Translate arrived as a phone app and inside web browsers, and machine translation went into everyday use. Today, the government's job handbook describes the translator's desk with both kinds of machine on it. Computer-assisted translation tools keep a database of sentences already translated. Localizers adapt the text and graphics of whole products for another language. Machine translation software writes a first draft, which translators then review, in a process called post-editing. The word from 1966 is back. Then, correcting the machine was slower than translating from scratch. Now the handbook lists it as part of the job.

[11]
The change shows up in the numbers too. Economists at Oxford compared six hundred ninety-five local labor markets in the United States. Where people used Google Translate more, translator employment grew more slowly. Overall, they estimate about twenty-eight thousand translator positions were never created. That is an estimate, not a count. Wages slowed at first, then recovered around 2016. And similar jobs, such as editors and technical writers, showed no such effect. The study also points to a shift toward higher-skilled translation work, the tasks machine translation cannot easily replace.

[12]
Interpreting has changed in its own way. Interpreters now work remotely as well as in person. And in courtrooms, where this story began, most states now require interpreters to be certified. Demand for American Sign Language interpreters is expected to grow, as more people use video relay services: online video calls with a sign language interpreter on the line. A diverse population, growing globalization, and the ongoing need for military and national security interpreters, are expected to add work too.

[13]
And the next ten years? The government projects that interpreters and translators will grow from seventy-three thousand nine hundred jobs in 2025 to seventy-five thousand four hundred in 2035. That is two percent, compared with three point five percent for all jobs. About six thousand openings are projected each year, mostly to replace people who retire or change careers. Computer tools, including AI, are making the work more efficient, the handbook says. But many of these jobs cannot be entirely automated, because computers cannot yet produce work comparable to what human translators do in most cases.

[14]
It is not the same technology as in 1954, but the pattern is familiar: a repetitive part of the work goes to a machine, and people move to the work around it. One difference matters. In 1966, the machine was not good enough, and the committee said so. This time, it is good enough for everyday use, and one study finds it has slowed the growth of translator jobs. The word yet in that sentence is the open question: how much of the work will stay with people, and which part?

[15]
In 1954, the forecast was five years. It took more than fifty. When the machines finally could translate everyday text, the people did not disappear. The work moved: from waiting for a speaker to stop, to speaking at the same time; from writing every sentence, to checking a machine's draft; from the courtroom, to a video call. What stays with people, for now, is the part where a wrong word matters.

[16]
Back on the street, the lamplighter reaches the last post. The glass opens, the flame takes, and the light passes on to the next corner, the way the work does. Every era has its last lamplighters. Some of them carried a sentence from one language to another.
