S1 — The trial that changed interpreting
In October 1945, Leon Dostert, General Eisenhower's wartime interpreter, set up the interpretation for the war crimes tribunal at Nuremberg. He chose simultaneous interpretation, a method many thought would not work, and IBM donated the equipment. Full film: "From Headphones to Machine Drafts: How Translators' Work Changed" on the channel.
Sources: Hutchins, "The first public demonstration of machine translation: the Georgetown-IBM system"; Bowen & Bowen, "The Nuremberg Trials (Communication through Translation)", Meta. #history #translation #interpreting

S2 — 1954: a machine that translated 250 words
On January 7, 1954, a Georgetown team and IBM showed a computer translating Russian sentences in New York. It knew only 250 words and six rules of grammar, and the forecasts said a translating machine could be ready within a few years. Full film on the channel.
Source: Hutchins, "The first public demonstration of machine translation: the Georgetown-IBM system, 7th January 1954". #history #translation #computers

S3 — A 1966 word for translators is back
Machine translation software writes a first draft, which the translator then reviews: the government's job handbook calls this post-editing, the same word a 1966 committee used for people correcting the machine. Then, it was slower than translating from scratch. Now it is part of the job. Full film on the channel.
Sources: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Occupational Outlook Handbook: Interpreters and Translators; ALPAC, "Language and Machines" (National Academy of Sciences, 1966). #translation #jobs #AI
