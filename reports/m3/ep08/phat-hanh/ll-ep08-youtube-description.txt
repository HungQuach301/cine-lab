TITLE
From Headphones to Machine Drafts: How Translators' Work Changed

DESCRIPTION
In 1954, a computer in New York translated more than sixty Russian sentences, and the forecasts said machines would translate almost anything within a few years. It took more than fifty. This film follows how the work of translators and interpreters changed along the way: from the headphones and glass booths of the Nuremberg tribunal in 1945, where simultaneous interpretation was first used on a large scale, to a 1966 committee that found machine output had to be corrected by people, a job it called postediting.

Today, machine translation is in everyday use, and post-editing a machine's draft is part of the translator's job. Oxford economists estimate about 28,000 translator jobs that the profession's growth would have created were never added: an estimate, not a count of layoffs. The BLS projects interpreters and translators to grow from 73,900 jobs in 2025 to 75,400 in 2035, 2 percent, slower than the 3.5 percent projected for all jobs.

The street and its lamplighter are fictional. Every number on screen comes from the sources listed below. Archival photographs: Library of Congress, Farm Security Administration/Office of War Information collection (no known restrictions).
