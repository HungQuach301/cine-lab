[00 HOOK]

Midnight at the Gibraltar frontier. To walk in from Spain, you cross an airport runway.

Since July 2026, this gate has had no routine checks.

The Rock sits at the western gate of the Mediterranean.

Historians on both sides count fourteen sieges of it, between 1309 and 1783.

This is why everyone fought over it, told from both sides' records.

In 1782, ten floating batteries came to break it.

In 1941, a runway was laid across the only road to Spain.

So why, today, is the fight over it about paperwork and payrolls?

[01 ROCK]

Austria, England and the Dutch backed a rival for Spain's crown; France backed Madrid's king.

That summer, their fleet sailed into the Bay of Gibraltar.

Its commanders, Admiral George Rooke and Prince George of Hesse-Darmstadt, fought for the rival claimant, "Charles the Third."

This plan, printed in 1705, shows the prize: a walled town that historians on both sides would call the key of the Mediterranean.

Inside, Governor Diego de Salinas had barely a hundred soldiers, by a Spanish count. He refused to surrender. The fleet answered with some fifteen thousand rounds, by British and Spanish accounts alike.

On the fourth of August 1704, by the new calendar, Gibraltar surrendered.

British accounts put the defenders at up to five hundred, counting armed townsmen.

Townspeople could stay if they swore loyalty to "Charles the Third." By a British account, almost all left; by a Spanish one, the largest group settled a league north-east, at San Roque.

In 1713, the Treaty of Utrecht gave it to the British Crown "for ever," but "without any territorial jurisdiction, and without any open communication by land." If Britain ever gave it up, Spain would have first refusal.

The treaty names the town, castle and port, not the sandy isthmus to the north. Spain says the isthmus was never ceded. Britain said in 2015 that Gibraltar includes it.

Within weeks, Spain and France besieged it, into 1705. Spain tried again in 1727. Neither siege took it.

So in 1730, Philip the Fifth ordered a stone line across the isthmus, out of gun range, to cut Gibraltar off by land. The town behind it is still called La Línea: "the line."

[02 SIEGE]

Half a century later came a different question: can you starve a rock? On the twenty-first of June 1779, Madrid cut Gibraltar off from Spain.

Spain had joined France in its war with Britain. By October, British intelligence put the Spanish army facing the Rock at up to fourteen thousand.

At sea, from mid-July, a Spanish squadron sealed the port. Based at Algeciras, its boats were there to cut off the food reaching the Rock from Africa.

By January 1780, thistles, dandelions and wild leeks were daily food for many; an egg cost sixpence.

The governor, General Eliott, could hold the walls. The food had to come by sea.

To break the blockade, Britain sent three relief fleets: 1780, 1781, 1782.

Scurvy spread, until a Danish ship of lemons was brought in, garrison diaries record.

On the twelfth of April 1781, the second relief convoy dropped anchor, and Spain's batteries opened fire on the town. The townspeople fled south.

Two days later, soldiers broke into the merchants' stores. A British officer blamed "resentment and intoxication."

By British tallies, the two sides fired over four hundred and sixty thousand rounds.

By September 1782, the garrison counted about seven and a half thousand men.

The garrison turned to heat: cannonballs heated red-hot, so that whatever they hit caught fire. On the eighth of September, they used them in earnest against the Spanish works.

Four days later, the French and Spanish fleets gathered in the bay: forty-seven ships of the line by the British count, fifty by the Spanish. The Duc de Crillon led the siege.

Their answer to red-hot shot: ten floating batteries, designed by the French engineer d'Arçon. A French account says he'd asked to test them against red-hot shot first. It was refused.

The thirteenth of September 1782. That morning, the batteries moved into position under the garrison's fire. At ten twenty-five, by the Spanish account, the attackers' guns, some four hundred, opened fire together. By the next afternoon, all ten batteries were lost.

Over two thousand attackers were lost, by a British estimate that includes prisoners, and by a Spanish one; the British rescued three hundred and fifty-seven.

Before the attack, all summer, the garrison had been digging through the north face. By early July, Sergeant Ince's thirteen men had cut about eighty-two feet of solid rock.

In July they blasted a hole for air. A Spanish historian wrote it was big enough for a cannon.

The blockade never forced a surrender. In February 1783, word came of a preliminary peace, and on the fifth, the last shot was fired.

Spanish historian Montero put losses near two thousand defenders and, from all causes, six thousand besiegers. The garrison's records count fewer dead: at least eight hundred and sixty-nine. Gibraltar stayed British.

[03 NEUTRAL]

Which leaves a question the treaty never answered: where does the Rock end? Between the walls and the Spanish line lay a strip of sand: the neutral ground.

Around 1810, with Napoleon's armies heading for the Strait, Spain and Britain were allies. With Spain's consent, the British blew up the Spanish forts.

After that, the edge kept moving. A Spanish historian describes Britain advancing into the neutral zone step by step.

On the Spanish side, a town grew up behind the old line. In 1870, it became La Línea de la Concepción.

Remember this street in La Línea. We'll come back to it.

This was the gap between them: a strip of neutral ground between two towns.

In 1908, Britain told Spain, "as an act of courtesy," that it would fence the edge of the neutral ground. The fence went up in 1909; Spain does not recognise it as a border.

Behind the fence: a garrison camp, gardens, rifle ranges, a racecourse.

And from 1934, a grass airstrip was built there. Subscribe if you'd like the next place in this series.

[04 RUNWAY]

Why build an airfield on disputed ground? In March 1939, Spain protested that the works stood on "a usurped portion" of its territory.

That November, Spain's foreign minister called the strip a fait accompli, like the Rock itself, while insisting its use breached Spanish territory. Late in 1939, Britain began a hard runway; in 1941, it was laid across the only road to Spain.

Then Britain pushed it out into the bay, with stone blasted from the north face and rubble from new tunnels.

Inside the Rock, those tunnels held a road about a mile long, a hospital and a power station, dug by British and Canadian tunnellers.

Gibraltar counts about fifty-five kilometres of tunnels from every era, more than its roads above ground.

This 1943 US Army map shows the town house by house, and the runway reaching into the bay.

May 1940: Gibraltar sends its women and children away. June: Spain turns "non-belligerent" and occupies Tangier. By 1942, the invasion of North Africa is run from inside the Rock.

For the rest of the war, a Spanish scholar found, Spain protested what it called breaches of Spanish waters; no complaint over the airstrip is on record.

On the night of the seventh of November 1942, by Gibraltar's official record, at least six hundred and fifty aircraft lined the runway. The invasion fleets, from Britain and the United States, came through the Strait.

[05 GATE]

After the war, the argument returned to the gate. In June 1969, Spain closed the land frontier completely. It followed Gibraltar's new constitution; a Spanish historian says Madrid applied Utrecht strictly.

Britain counted some four thousand six hundred Spanish day workers cut off. The gate fully reopened in 1985.

The same square, today. In October 2025, people living in Spain held more than four in ten of Gibraltar's jobs.

The UK government puts the daily crossers higher, at about fifteen thousand. People on foot still cross the runway.

And on the other side of the gate.

Remember this street? In 2024, La Línea's unemployment was twenty-eight point six percent: the highest of a hundred and thirteen Spanish cities.

Here's the twist. Gibraltar's government puts output per resident at about eighty-five thousand pounds.

That spreads output over the thirty-eight thousand who live there, and leaves out the people in Spain who hold fourteen thousand of its jobs. Our read: part of the work behind that number is done from the other side.

In July 2026, Britain and the EU signed a treaty; the next day, routine checks here ended.

It says it changes neither side's position on sovereignty. In July, Gibraltar's government said the old fence was coming down, with a new one a few metres behind; only about a hundred and fifty metres would stay open.

As of 18 October 2026, the treaty is in force only provisionally, pending a European Parliament vote indicated for the fourteenth of December.

So why did everyone fight over this place? Where it sits, and the sea that could feed it. A wall in 1704. A line in 1730. A fence in 1909. A runway in 1941.
