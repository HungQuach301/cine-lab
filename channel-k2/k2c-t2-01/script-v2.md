In 1943, at the peak, America launched three cargo ships a day. {C03} One California yard, in a publicity stunt, built a ship in under five days. {C04}

In a 2026 report, Congress's researchers put America at four hundredths of one percent of world shipbuilding tonnage. China, in 2024: more than half. {C05}

And the company planning to expand a Philadelphia shipyard is South Korean. {C40}

So why does America build so few ships? You'll often hear: wages. The record says something narrower.

[01 KAISER]
Start in Richmond, California, in December 1940. Henry J. Kaiser started his first shipyard in Richmond, on the bay. {C07} What made it possible was an order: ships for the British government, already at war. {C08}

The ship America chose to mass-produce was the Liberty: a plain cargo ship with a top speed of about eleven knots, designed as an emergency answer to a shortage of ships. {C09} Yards built it the way Detroit built cars, on an assembly line. {C10}

In June 1942, a Liberty ship took a hundred and twenty-two days, on average. {C11} Later, as a publicity stunt, one of the Richmond yards built the Robert E. Peary in four days and fifteen and a half hours. {C04} By December 1943, the average across all Liberty yards was thirty-nine days. {C11}

Over 2,710 Liberty ships were built between 1941 and 1945. {C12} The four Richmond yards alone produced 747 ships of all kinds. {C13} Richmond grew from fewer than twenty-four thousand people in 1940 to nearly a hundred thousand by 1943, more than its housing, roads and schools could hold. {C14} Across the country, almost eighty yards held Maritime Commission contracts, and at the peak they employed 702,000 people. {C15}

Notice who the customers were: the British government, at war, and America's own Maritime Commission. {C08} {C15} Governments, in wartime.

[02 JONES]
War orders end. After April 1944, the Maritime Commission let no more contracts for Liberty ships. {C16}

America already had a different kind of customer, written into law. In the Merchant Marine Act of 1920, Senator Wesley Jones of Washington, chair of the Commerce Committee, sought to make cargo bound for Alaska move through the Port of Seattle. {C17} Today, Section 27 of that act is called the Jones Act. It says a ship carrying cargo from one American port to another must be American-built, and owned and crewed by Americans. {C18}

Congress's researchers say the act gives American shipyards "a significant degree of protection." {C18} Supporters, like the American Maritime Partnership, say it protects American jobs and American security. {C47} Critics, like Senator Mike Lee and Representative Tom McClintock, who filed a bill in 2025 to repeal it, say its costs mean "higher prices for Americans." {C48}

There was also a federal subsidy, meant to let American owners buy American ships at the world price. {C20} In the 1970s, American yards built about five percent of the world's tonnage: fifteen to twenty-five new ships a year. {C21} The subsidy ended in the 1980s. Congress's researchers note that some observers see its end as the main reason fewer ships were built that decade. {C21} Output fell to around five ships a year, which a 2023 congressional report calls the current rate. {C21}

Now the answer you'll often hear: American shipyards lost because American workers cost too much. {C22}

Here's the record. The cost gap is a century old. A 1922 government report put American-built ships at twenty percent more than foreign ones. By the 1950s, double. By the 1990s, three times. {C23} In 2019, Congress's researchers cited one maritime consulting firm's estimate: an American-built tanker costs about four times the world price. {C24}

Those researchers do list wages, especially for welders, among the reasons. Currency policy, too. Then they add one more: without exports, American shipyards can't reach economies of scale — the savings that come from building many of the same thing. {C25}

Our read: the protected home market is small. In March 2018, the Jones Act fleet had ninety-nine oceangoing ships. {C26} And at home, the same researchers note, the high price of American-built ships holds buyers back wherever there's another way to move cargo, like a truck, a train or a pipeline. {C46}

Put those together and you get a loop. High prices mean few orders. Few orders mean no scale. No scale keeps prices high. That loop is our read; each piece is in the record. So the record says something narrower than "wages": wages are one piece, and building a few ships a year for one market is another.

[04 CHUNG]
Now cross the Pacific. In 1962, South Korea's output was about a hundred and seven dollars per person, in that year's dollars. {C27}

Chung Ju-yung had built Hyundai as a construction company. When he decided to go into shipbuilding in the early 1970s, by the company's own account Hyundai had "no experience, capital, or shipbuilding technology whatsoever." {C28}

By Hyundai's account, while the shipyard was still being planned, a Greek shipowner, George Livanos, ordered two crude oil tankers. {C29} A 2010 Korea Times column tells the rest. Chung went abroad carrying a photo of the empty beach and a 500-won banknote showing a turtle ship, a warship from Korea's history. {C30} The column says a consortium of English banks financed the equipment. {C30} We found no loan documents, so treat the banknote as lore: one columnist's account, not a record.

Hyundai's own history says ground was broken in March 1972 on that beach in Ulsan, for what it calls the world's largest shipyard. {C31} By the company's account, it built the shipyard and the two tankers at the same time, and finished both in 1974. {C06}

Hyundai wasn't alone. In January 1973, President Park Chung Hee announced a drive for heavy industry, naming shipbuilding, with exports as the goal. {C49} An economist writing for the Korea Economic Institute of America lists the support: tax incentives, subsidized long-term loans, and industrial complexes built by the government. {C49}

Its first customer was a foreign owner. Our read: from the start, this yard was built to sell abroad. In 2025, South Korea's output was about thirty-six thousand dollars per person, again in that year's dollars, not adjusted for inflation. {C27} The sources we used don't measure how much of that rise came from shipbuilding.

[05 CHINA]
In 1999, China built less than five percent of the world's ship tonnage. By 2023, over half. {C32} In 2024, 54.6 percent. {C05}

The U.S. Trade Representative found that Chinese government plans set production and market-share targets, and backed them with subsidies and state financing. Some studies estimate that support at ninety-one billion dollars from 2006 to 2013. {C33} China's Ministry of Commerce rejected the U.S. measures. In February 2025, its spokesperson said they would "fail to revitalize" American shipbuilding and raise shipping costs. {C50}

Chinese yards also built mainly for export. Congress's researchers say ninety percent of the ships Chinese shipbuilders made in 2025 were for export. {C34}

So, the honest part. Korea and China both used state support, and the evidence on whether that pays is mixed. That same economist found Korea's targeted industries grew faster after 1973, but their productivity didn't rise significantly. {C51} And no source we found measures how much of America's decline came from the subsidy ending, how much from wages, and how much from scale. {C36}

[07 NOW]
Which brings us to the present. In January 2025, the Trade Representative found China's targeting of shipbuilding "unreasonable" and a burden on U.S. commerce. {C52} From October fourteenth, 2025, ships owned or operated by Chinese companies owed a fee of fifty dollars per net ton; most other China-built ships owed eighteen, or a per-container fee if higher. {C38} Less than a month later, the Trade Representative suspended the fees until November ninth, 2026. {C01} In September 2026, more than two hundred trade associations asked for the suspension to be extended. Resuming the fees, they wrote, "would add another cost layer to an already strained transportation system." {C39} As of October fourteenth, 2026, no extension had been published. {C01}

And Philadelphia. According to Korea JoongAng Daily, Hanwha, a South Korean group, bought the shipyard for a hundred million dollars in December 2024. {C40} In August 2025, it announced five billion dollars of investment, with a goal of raising output from one and a half ships a year to twenty. That's an announced goal, not yet built. {C40} The month before, South Korea's government had proposed a hundred-and-fifty-billion-dollar shipbuilding fund as part of a tariff deal; details weren't public then. In July 2026, the two governments opened a cooperation center for it in Washington. {C41} {C53} And that same month, Military Times reported the Philadelphia yard had been tapped to build two missile-tracking ships for the Pentagon, the first slated for June 2030. {C42}

Our read: the pattern again — an order. This time it came after the investment was announced, but before the new capacity exists.
