[00 HOOK]
In 1943, at the peak, America launched three cargo ships a day.

In a 2026 report, Congress's researchers put America's share of world shipbuilding tonnage at 0.04 percent. China's, in 2024: more than half.

And the company that has announced plans to expand a Philadelphia shipyard is South Korean.

So why does America build so few ships? You'll often hear: wages. The record says something narrower. And there's a deadline in this story: November ninth, 2026.

[01 KAISER]
Start in Richmond, California, in December 1940, when Henry J. Kaiser started his first Richmond shipyard. What allowed it to grow, the Park Service says, were orders: ships for the British government, already at war.

The Liberty was a slow cargo ship, designed as an emergency answer to a shortage of cargo ships.

In June 1942, a Liberty ship took a hundred and twenty-two days, on average. By December 1943, thirty-nine. As a publicity stunt, one Richmond yard built the Robert E. Peary in four days and fifteen and a half hours.

In 1943, a government photographer pictured one of the people building Liberty ships in Richmond: Josie Lucille Owens, a welder trainee, helping build the Liberty ship George Washington Carver.

The four Richmond yards produced 747 ships, more than any other shipyard complex in the country. Richmond grew from fewer than twenty-four thousand people in 1940 to nearly a hundred thousand by 1943.

Our read: notice who the customers were. The British government, and America's own Maritime Commission. Governments, in wartime.

[02 JONES]
In 1944, the Liberty program was cut back. After April, no more Liberty contracts were let.

America already had a different kind of customer, written into law in 1920. Back then, American shippers were sending Alaska cargo by rail to Vancouver, Canada, and on by foreign ships. Senator Wesley Jones of Washington, chair of the Commerce Committee, sought to stop that by requiring Alaska-bound cargo to move through the Port of Seattle. The cargo rule in that act, Section 27, is known as the Jones Act: a ship carrying cargo between two American ports must be American-built, and owned and crewed by Americans.

Congress's researchers say it gives American shipyards "a significant degree of protection." The American Maritime Partnership, a coalition of supporters, puts its case in its headline: "American Jobs. American Security." Critics, like Senator Mike Lee and Representative Tom McClintock, who introduced a bill on June twelfth, 2025, to repeal it, say its costs mean "higher prices for Americans."

There was also a federal subsidy, the Construction Differential Subsidy, meant to let American owners buy American ships at the world price. In the 1970s, American yards built about five percent of the world's tonnage: fifteen to twenty-five new ships a year. The subsidy ended in the 1980s, and Congress's researchers note that some observers see that as the main reason fewer ships were built. Output fell to around five a year, which a 2023 congressional report calls the current rate.

[03 MYTH]
Now the answer you'll often hear: American shipyards lost because American workers cost too much.

The cost gap goes back at least to 1922, when a government report put American-built ships at twenty percent more than foreign ones. By the 1950s, double. By the 1990s, three times. In 2019, Congress's researchers cited one consulting firm's estimate for one ship type: an American-built tanker, at about four times the global price of a similar vessel.

A 2023 report from Congress's researchers lists wages, especially for welders, among the factors behind higher prices. Then it adds one more: without exports, American shipyards can't reach economies of scale — the savings that come from building many of the same thing.

Our read: the protected home market is small. A 2019 congressional report counted ninety-nine oceangoing ships in the Jones Act fleet as of March 2018. At home, the 2023 report adds, high prices hold buyers back wherever a truck, a train or a pipeline can do the job.

Our read: put those together and you get a loop. High prices mean few orders. Few orders mean no scale. No scale keeps prices high. The pieces come from the record; the loop is our interpretation. Our emphasis on scale is a position the evidence only partly supports.

[04 CHUNG]
In 1962, South Korea's output was about a hundred and seven dollars per person, in that year's dollars.

Chung Ju-yung had built Hyundai as a construction company. When he decided to go into shipbuilding in the early 1970s, by the company's own account Hyundai had "no experience, capital, or shipbuilding technology whatsoever."

By Hyundai's account, while the shipyard was still being planned, a Greek shipowner, George Livanos, ordered two crude oil tankers. So, by that account, an order from a foreign owner came before the yard existed.

A 2010 Korea Times column adds a story. Chung went abroad carrying a photo of the empty beach and a 500-won banknote showing a turtle ship, a warship from Korea's history. We found no documents for it, so treat it as lore: one columnist's account, not a record.

Hyundai's own history says ground was broken in March 1972 on that beach in Ulsan, for what it calls the world's largest shipyard. By the company's account, it completed the shipyard and two supertankers, the Atlantic Baron and the Atlantic Baroness, at the same time, in 1974.

Meanwhile, the government set its own targets. In January 1973, President Park Chung Hee announced a drive for heavy industry, naming shipbuilding, with exports as the goal. Munseob Lee, writing for the Korea Economic Institute of America in 2023, lists the support: tax incentives, subsidized long-term loans, and industrial complexes built by the government.

Our read: from the start, this yard's customers were abroad. In 2025, South Korea's output was about thirty-six thousand dollars per person, again in that year's dollars, not adjusted for inflation. The sources we used don't measure how much of that rise came from shipbuilding.

[05 NOW]
Which brings us to the present. In March 2024, five American labor unions, including the steelworkers and the boilermakers, petitioned the U.S. Trade Representative over China's shipbuilding.

Congress's researchers summarize the Trade Representative's findings: Chinese government plans set production and market-share targets, backed by subsidies and state financing. And in 2025, ninety percent of the ships Chinese shipbuilders made were for export.

In January 2025, the Trade Representative found that China's "targeted dominance" in these sectors was "unreasonable" and "burdens or restricts U.S. commerce."

Then came the fees. From October 2025, ships owned or operated by Chinese companies, and ships built in China, owed a fee on their U.S. voyages, based mostly on the ship's size, up to five times a year. The stated aim: to discourage the use and purchase of Chinese-built ships, and to press China to drop the practices the Trade Representative had found. China's Ministry of Commerce had already objected: in February 2025, responding to the proposed measures, a spokesperson said they would "fail to revitalize" American shipbuilding and raise shipping costs.

Within a month, "in light of" a trade deal between President Trump and President Xi, the notice says, the Trade Representative suspended them through November ninth, 2026. In September, a letter from more than two hundred trade groups, by our count of its signatories, asked for the suspension to be extended. Resuming the fees, they wrote, "would add another cost layer to an already strained transportation system." As of October fifteenth, 2026, no extension had been published.

And Philadelphia. According to Korea JoongAng Daily, Hanwha, a South Korean group, bought the shipyard for a hundred million dollars in December 2024. In August 2025, it announced five billion dollars of investment, with a goal of raising output from one and a half ships a year to twenty. That's an announced goal; the article gives no date for reaching it. As of October fifteenth, 2026: in July, Military Times reported the Philadelphia yard had been tapped to build two missile-tracking ships for the Pentagon, the first slated for June 2030.

Our read: the pattern again, an order. This time it came after the investment was announced, and before any report we found of the new capacity being finished.

[06 END]
So, what to watch. November ninth, 2026: whether the fee pause is extended or runs out as the notice sets. Philadelphia: whether the announced investment shows up as new capacity. And June 2030: the delivery date Military Times reported for the first of those ships.

The scoreboard. 1943, at the peak: three Liberty ships launched a day. A 2023 congressional report: around five new ships a year. Different ships, different eras.

Our read of the record: America didn't forget how to build ships. Since the 1980s, it has built few because few orders came — and the record ties high prices to fewer domestic purchases, and the lack of exports to missing economies of scale. No source we found measures how much each factor counts.
