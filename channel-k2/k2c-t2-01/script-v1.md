On November ninth, a one-year pause ends. Unless Washington extends it, ships built or run by Chinese companies will again owe a fee at American ports. {C01} {C38} The stated goal: rebuild American shipbuilding. {C02}

Yet in 1943, America launched three cargo ships a day. {C03} One California yard built a ship in four and a half days. {C04}

Today, Congress's researchers put America at four hundredths of one percent of the world's shipbuilding tonnage. China: more than half. {C05}

The company expanding a Philadelphia shipyard is from South Korea, where in 1972 a firm with no shipbuilding experience broke ground on an empty beach. {C40} {C28} {C31}

So why can't America build ships anymore? Most people say wages. The record says something else.

This video explains how the system works. It isn't financial or investment advice. Facts are as of October fourteenth, 2026.

[01 KAISER]
Start in Richmond, California, in December 1940. Henry J. Kaiser started his first shipyard in Richmond, on the bay. {C07} What made it possible was an order: ships for Britain, already at war. {C08}

The ship America chose to mass-produce was the Liberty: a slow, plain cargo ship, designed as an emergency answer to a shortage of ships. {C09} Yards built it like a car on an assembly line. {C10}

In June 1942, a Liberty ship took a hundred and twenty-two days, on average. {C11} That November, as a publicity stunt, Kaiser's Yard Number Two built the Robert E. Peary in four days and fifteen and a half hours. {C04} By December 1943, the average was thirty-nine days. {C11}

Over 2,710 Liberty ships were built between 1941 and 1945. {C12} The four Richmond yards alone produced 747 ships. {C13} Kaiser's yards hired a diverse workforce, including women and minorities, challenging the norms of the day. {C45} Richmond itself grew from fewer than twenty-four thousand people in 1940 to nearly a hundred thousand by 1943, more than its housing, roads and schools could hold. {C14} Across the country, yards with government contracts employed 702,000 people at the peak. {C15}

Notice who the customers were: first the British government, then America's own Maritime Commission. {C08} {C15} Governments at war.

[02 JONES]
And war orders end. In April 1944, the government signed its last contract for Liberty ships. {C16}

America already had a different kind of customer, written into law. In the Merchant Marine Act of 1920, Senator Wesley Jones of Washington, chair of the Commerce Committee, pushed a change to the rules for shipping between American ports. {C17} That section, Section 27, now called the Jones Act, says a ship carrying cargo from one American port to another must be American-built, and American-owned and crewed. {C18}

That gives American yards a customer foreign yards can't touch: the home routes. {C19} There was also a federal subsidy meant to let American owners buy American ships at the world price. {C20}

In the 1970s, American yards still built about five percent of the world's tonnage: fifteen to twenty-five new ships a year. Then the subsidy ended in the 1980s. Some observers see that as the main reason fewer ships were built. And output fell to around five ships a year. That's roughly the rate today. {C21}

[03 MYTH]
If you've found this useful so far, subscribing is how you'll catch the next one.

Now the common answer. You'll often hear that American shipyards lost because American workers cost too much. {C22}

Here's the record. Cost has been a problem for a century. One 1922 government report put American-built ships at twenty percent more than foreign ones. By the 1950s, double. By the 1990s, three times. {C23} Today, one maritime consulting firm estimates an American-built tanker costs about four times the world price. {C24}

Congress's researchers do list wages, especially for welders, among the factors. They list currency policy, too. But they add something else: without exports, American shipyards can't reach economies of scale. {C25} And the protected home market is small. In 2018, the Jones Act fleet had ninety-nine oceangoing ships. {C26} At home, the same researchers note, buyers are held back by the high price of American-built ships wherever there's an alternative, like a truck, a train or a pipeline. {C46}

Put those together and you get a loop. High prices mean few orders. Few orders mean no scale. No scale keeps prices high. That loop is our read; the pieces are in the record.

So the record says something narrower than "wages." Our read: wages are one piece. Building a few ships a year, for one market, is the other.

[04 CHUNG]
Now cross the Pacific. In 1962, South Korea's output was about a hundred and seven dollars per person, in that year's dollars. {C27}

Chung Ju-yung had built Hyundai as a construction company. {C28} When he went into shipbuilding in the early 1970s, in the company's own telling, it had "no experience, capital, or shipbuilding technology whatsoever." {C28}

What it had first was an order. While the shipyard was still being planned, a Greek shipowner, George Livanos, ordered two crude oil tankers. {C29} As a Korea Times column told it in 2010, Chung went looking for money carrying a photo of an empty beach and a 500-won banknote with a picture of a turtle ship, a famous warship from Korea's past. {C30} Technical help came from the Scott Lithgow shipyard in Scotland, and a consortium of English banks agreed to finance the equipment. {C30} We found no loan documents, so treat the banknote as the story Korea tells about itself.

In March 1972, ground was broken on that beach in Ulsan, for what would become the world's largest shipyard. {C31} Hyundai built the shipyard and the two tankers at the same time, and finished both in 1974. {C06}

Look at where those first ships were going: to a foreign owner. From the start, the yard was selling to the whole world. In 2025, South Korea's output was about thirty-six thousand dollars per person. {C27}

[05 CHINA]
Then came a bigger version of the same move. In 1999, China built less than five percent of the world's ship tonnage. By 2023, over half. {C32} By 2024, 54.6 percent. {C05}

According to Congress's researchers, the U.S. trade representative found that Chinese government plans set production and market-share targets, and backed them with subsidies and state financing. Some studies estimate that support at ninety-one billion dollars from 2006 to 2013. {C33}

And like Ulsan, China built for the world. Congress's researchers say ninety percent of the ships China made in 2025 were for export. By 2024, China led production in every category of ship but one, gas carriers. {C34}

[06 HONEST]
Now the honest part. These numbers come in different units. Shares here are by tonnage, not ship counts, and two congressional reports, two years apart, put America at 0.2 percent and 0.04 percent. {C35} The fourfold cost is one consulting firm's estimate, quoted by Congress's researchers. {C24}

And no source we found measures how much of the decline came from the subsidy ending, how much from wages, and how much from scale. {C36} Defenders of the Jones Act point out it gives American yards and carriers a significant degree of protection. {C18} Critics point to the cost. Both are in the record.

[07 NOW]
Which brings us back to this year. In April 2025, an executive order said America builds less than one percent of the world's commercial ships, and set a policy to "revitalize and rebuild domestic maritime industries." {C37}

Then came the fees. From October fourteenth, 2025, ships owned or run by Chinese companies owed fifty dollars per net ton to enter a U.S. port; Chinese-built ships owed eighteen. {C38} Less than a month later, the fees were suspended until November ninth, 2026. {C01} In September, more than two hundred trade groups asked for another extension. Resuming the fees, they wrote, "would add another cost layer to an already strained transportation system." {C39} As of October fourteenth, none had been published. {C01}

And the Philadelphia shipyard? Hanwha, a South Korean group, bought it for a reported hundred million dollars in December 2024. {C40} In August 2025, it announced five billion dollars of investment to raise output from one and a half ships a year to twenty. {C40} That came weeks after Seoul offered a hundred-and-fifty-billion-dollar shipbuilding fund in a trade deal; details were still unknown at the time. {C41} In July 2026, the yard was picked to build two missile-tracking ships for the Pentagon. The first is due in June 2030. {C42}

There it is again. An order, before the expansion.

[08 END]
So, what to watch. November ninth: whether the fee pause ends. Philadelphia: whether the new docks get built. And 2030: the first of those new ships.

Here's the scoreboard. 1943: three Liberty ships launched a day. {C03} Recently: around five a year. {C21} Our read of the record: America didn't forget how to build ships. For four decades, it mostly stopped getting the orders.

Back in Richmond, one of Kaiser's shipyards is now part of a national park. {C43}
