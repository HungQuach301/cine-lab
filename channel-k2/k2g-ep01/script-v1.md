[00 HOOK]
In 1950, America made almost half of the world's steel. In 2025, it made nearly as much — and that was about four percent. So why is steel now guarded like a weapon?

[01 CONTEXT]
This video explains how the system works. It isn't financial or investment advice.

You rarely notice steel, and you are almost never far from it: the frame of the building around you, the rebar in the road, the body of the car. Our read: a country can import its phones and still function. A country that can't make steel at scale has to buy the skeleton of everything big it builds from someone else.

This story has three layers. The old one: how one river valley in Pennsylvania learned to make steel cheaper than almost anyone. The recent one: how governments, from Washington to Brussels, started treating steel imports as a security question. And the new one: a price now attached to steel crossing one major border — the price of its carbon.

Each layer runs on the same machine underneath. By the end, you'll see what it is.

[02 LAYER1 SITE]
Layer one. Pittsburgh, the 1870s.

Andrew Carnegie was not a steelmaker by training. He was a Scottish immigrant who had risen through a telegraph office and the Pennsylvania Railroad. In England, he had seen the Bessemer process demonstrated: a way to make steel in bulk.

Railroads would need rails. Where to make them? In his autobiography, Carnegie describes waking one Sunday morning with the answer: a site called Braddock's, on the Monongahela River, "between the Pennsylvania, the Baltimore and Ohio, and the river." Two railroads and a river. Three ways in, three ways out.

The land cost more than he hoped: ground they expected to buy at five or six hundred dollars an acre, he wrote, cost them two thousand. He built anyway, and named the works after his friend Edgar Thomson, president of the Pennsylvania Railroad. When the Edgar Thomson works began, Carnegie recalled, steel rails sold for about seventy dollars a ton.

[03 LAYER1 CHAIN]
Now look at what Carnegie was actually assembling, because this is the first piece of the machine.

In his autobiography, he wrote down the whole chain behind a single ton of steel. One and a half tons of iron ore: mined, carried by rail about a hundred miles to the Great Lakes, then by boat for hundreds of miles, then by rail again, a hundred and fifty miles, to Pittsburgh. One and a half tons of coal, turned into coke and carried fifty-odd miles. And a ton of limestone, carried a hundred and fifty miles.

Four tons of material, moved hundreds of miles, to make one ton of steel. And at the end of all that, Carnegie marveled, steel was being sold at "three pounds for two cents."

That isn't a secret formula. It's geography plus logistics. Our read: whoever controls the cheapest path from rock to rail gets to set the price everyone else has to meet.

[04 LAYER1 CONTROL]
So Carnegie started buying the path.

Coke was a bottleneck. The best coking coal sat in one field, Connellsville. In 1882, Carnegie's firm bought half the stock of the Frick Coke Company, run by Henry Frick, a man Carnegie described as having started out as a poor railway clerk. Later purchases gave them most of the rest. By 1897, Carnegie wrote, that company held forty-two thousand acres of coal land, more than two-thirds of the Connellsville vein.

Then came railroad deals, and mills making, in Carnegie's words, "everything in steel from a wire nail up to a twenty-inch steel girder." Economists call this vertical integration: own each step, so nobody can charge you for theirs.

The same logic reached the people inside the mills. In 1892, at Carnegie's Homestead works, a strike turned into a battle on the riverbank. A Pennsylvania state historical marker records it this way: on orders of the Carnegie Steel Company, three hundred Pinkerton agents tried to land; strikers and townspeople drove them back; seven workers and three Pinkertons were killed. Eight thousand state militia arrived, and by November the strike was broken.

Our read: controlling the path also meant controlling the people who worked it, at a cost that no price list shows.

[05 LAYER1 POWER]
In March 1901, Carnegie wrote, the banker J. P. Morgan approached him through Charles Schwab and asked whether he really wished to retire from business. Carnegie said yes. A group led by Morgan and Elbert Gary bought Carnegie's company and combined it with their Federal Steel Company. The result was United States Steel, born in 1901 — the company at the center of layer two.

What did that machine mean for the country? According to the U.S. Geological Survey, the United States made about nine million tonnes of steel in 1900. In 1944, in the middle of the Second World War, it made about eighty-one million — roughly half of all the steel made on Earth that year. In 1950, still close to half.

That's what "steel as national power" meant in numbers. Not a slogan. A share: around mid-century, close to every other ton of the world's steel came out of American furnaces.

[06 MYTH1]
You'll often hear that America "stopped making steel." Let's put that line next to the record.

The U.S. Geological Survey's long series and the World Steel Association's latest figures say the United States made about eighty-eight million tonnes in 1950, and about eighty-two million in 2025. Not zero. Not half. Roughly the same.

What changed is everything around it. World production went from one hundred eighty-nine million tonnes in 1950 to more than one point eight billion in 2025 — almost ten times as much. China alone made about nine hundred sixty million tonnes in 2025. America's slice shrank from nearly half to about four percent, not mainly because American output collapsed, but because the rest of the world built far more.

How America makes steel changed too. In 2025, about seventy-one percent of U.S. steel came from electric arc furnaces, which can run largely on scrap. In China, it was about eleven percent. Hold on to those two numbers. They come back in layer three.

[07 LAYER2 TARIFFS]
Layer two. The recent past.

When your share of the world's steel falls from half to four percent, someone eventually asks: what happens in an emergency?

In January 2018, the U.S. Commerce Department answered under a 1962 law, Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act, which lets the president adjust imports that threaten to impair national security. The Secretary found that steel imports and global excess capacity were "weakening our internal economy." The report linked long-term viability to American mills running at about eighty percent of their capacity.

In March 2018, a proclamation put a twenty-five percent tariff on most imported steel. In February 2025, it was extended to imports from all countries. In June 2025, it was doubled, to fifty percent. In April 2026, the system was rebuilt around that fifty percent, with lower rates for many products made mostly of steel, and adjusted again in June.

Has it worked by its own yardstick? The April 2026 proclamation says steel capacity utilization rose from about seventy-two percent in 2017 to about seventy-seven percent — closer to the eighty percent goal, but not there yet. The proclamation credits the tariffs. Studies of earlier U.S. steel tariffs, summarized by the Congressional Research Service, found mixed effects on the steel industry and the industries that buy steel.

[08 LAYER2 OVERCAPACITY]
Why fifty percent? Look at the other side of the scale.

According to the OECD's Steel Outlook 2026, world steelmaking capacity reached a record of about two point four billion tonnes in 2025, while demand shrank for a fourth year in a row. The OECD projects the gap between what the world can make and what it uses to reach seven hundred forty-five million tonnes by 2028. When new mills can't sell at home, it notes, the steel is typically exported at low prices.

The OECD reports that Chinese steel exports hit a record one hundred thirty-one million tonnes in 2025. Put that next to the American figure: by weight, China's steel exports alone were larger than everything the United States produced. The OECD also reports that in 2024 the median Chinese steel firm received about fifteen times more in subsidies, relative to its assets, than the median producer elsewhere.

Europe built its own wall. From the first of July 2026, a new EU regulation replaced the old steel safeguard: import quotas of about eighteen point three million tonnes a year, and a fifty percent duty on anything above them.

Our read: the question moved from "who can make steel cheapest?" to "who is allowed to sell it here at all?"

[09 LAYER2 GOLDENSHARE]
And then the question reached the company Morgan had built.

In December 2023, U.S. Steel announced it would be acquired by Japan's Nippon Steel. After a national security review, on January 3, 2025, President Biden issued an order prohibiting the acquisition. On June 13, 2025, President Trump issued an order allowing it, on the condition of a national security agreement. Five days later, the deal closed.

Under that agreement, as the two companies described it, Nippon Steel committed about eleven billion dollars in new investment by 2028, and the headquarters stays in Pittsburgh. And U.S. Steel issued the U.S. government a "golden share."

A second popular claim worth checking: that the golden share means Washington now owns U.S. Steel. The companies' own announcement describes something narrower. The share lets the government appoint one independent director, and gives the president consent rights over specific decisions: cutting the committed investment, changing the name or headquarters, moving production or jobs out of the country, and certain plant closures or idlings. The full agreement has not been made public.

In September 2025, that power was used. The Associated Press reported that U.S. Steel had planned to stop processing steel slabs at its Granite City plant in Illinois. The White House said it intervened "with the threat of the president invoking his golden share authority." U.S. Steel reversed course.

More than a century ago, Carnegie bought the path from rock to rail. Today, a government holds a veto over parts of it.

[10 LAYER3 CARBON]
Layer three. The new product.

For most of its history, the price of steel was built from ore, energy and labor. A fourth ingredient is being added: carbon.

According to the World Steel Association, making a ton of steel the classic way — iron from a blast furnace, refined in a basic oxygen furnace — released on average about two point three tonnes of carbon dioxide, based on 2024 data. Melting scrap in an electric arc furnace released about zero point seven. Same product, different route, less than a third of the emissions.

Since January 1, 2026, the European Union has been turning that difference into a price at its border: the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism, or CBAM. Importers bringing more than fifty tonnes of covered goods into the EU, steel among them, must declare the emissions embedded in them and each year surrender certificates to cover them. The certificate price follows the EU's own carbon-market auctions. For goods imported between July and September 2026, the European Commission set it at eighty-two euros and thirty-two cents per tonne of carbon dioxide. Certificates go on sale in February 2027. If a carbon price was already paid where the steel was made, that amount can be deducted.

Our own calculation, as an illustration only: at that price, the average emissions of the blast-furnace route are worth about one hundred ninety euros per ton of steel; the scrap route, about fifty-seven. That is not what an importer actually pays in 2026, because the rules also adjust for free emission allowances European mills still receive. But it shows the direction: how a ton was made is starting to change what it costs to sell it in Europe.

Remember those furnace numbers: about seventy-one percent electric in the United States, about eleven in China. Our read: a carbon price at the border sorts steel by how it was made, not where — something a tariff doesn't do.

[11 LAYER3 HYDROGEN]
Then there's a route that skips coke entirely.

In August 2021, the Swedish steelmaker SSAB announced it had delivered the world's first steel made with iron reduced by fossil-free hydrogen instead of coal and coke: a trial delivery to the Volvo Group, from the HYBRIT partnership with LKAB and Vattenfall.

You'll hear two opposite claims: that green steel is already here, and that it's only marketing. The record sits in between. A trial delivery is real steel, but not yet an industry. A company called Stegra is building a large hydrogen-based steel plant in Boden, in northern Sweden. In June 2026 it closed a one point four billion euro financing round, and said its project timeline is under review. We won't guess when it opens.

Our read: a hydrogen route needs large amounts of clean electricity. As in Pittsburgh a hundred and fifty years ago, the new steel will be shaped by where its inputs are.

[12 THREAD]
So here is the machine underneath all three layers.

In the 1880s, power in steel meant controlling the cheapest path from rock to rail: ore, coke, limestone, railroads, river. Carnegie bought each piece of it.

In the 2020s, governments decided the path itself was a security question. Tariffs, quotas and a golden share all decide who gets to use it, and on what terms.

And now a new toll is being built into the path: the carbon each ton carries. Steel made with less of it faces a smaller charge at the European border.

Three layers. One question: who controls the path?

[13 END]
Back to where we started. In 1950, the United States made almost half the world's steel. In 2025, it made nearly the same tonnage, and that was about four percent.

So why guard it like a weapon? Our answer: because steel power was never only about tonnage. It was about share, and about control of the path. When America made half the world's steel, much of that path ran through Pittsburgh. Today, more of it runs through other countries' furnaces and policies. A tariff, a quota or a golden share is a government's attempt to keep a piece of that path at home.

Whether that is worth what it costs is a question these numbers alone can't settle. But the valley at Braddock is still there. The Edgar Thomson works, the mill Carnegie named in the 1870s, still makes steel today — for a company now owned by a Japanese steelmaker, under an agreement that gives an American president a veto over certain decisions.
