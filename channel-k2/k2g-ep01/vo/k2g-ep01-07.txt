Layer two. The recent past. Facts as of October 2026. In the United States, one legal route for treating steel imports as a national security question is Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962, under which the Commerce Secretary can find that imports "threaten to impair the national security," and the president can act on it. In March 2018, a proclamation under that law put a twenty-five percent tariff on imported steel, with Canada and Mexico exempted at first. It recorded the Commerce Secretary's finding that steel imports, together with global excess capacity, were "weakening our internal economy," and linked the industry's long-term viability to mills running at about eighty percent of capacity. In February 2025, a new proclamation applied it to all countries, ending earlier exemptions and alternative arrangements; the rate was doubled to fifty percent for most countries in June 2025, and restructured in April 2026. The latest change, as of October 2026, came in June 2026. The administration's April 2026 proclamation says that, in the Commerce Secretary's opinion, steel capacity utilization rose from about seventy-two percent in 2017 to about seventy-seven percent "at present" — that is, as of April 2026 — and it credits the tariffs. The Congressional Research Service notes that studies of the 2002 and 2018 U.S. steel tariffs reached different conclusions: some found the 2002 tariffs hurt the competitiveness and employment of American manufacturers that use steel; another suggested that foreign suppliers cut their prices in response to the 2018 tariffs, limiting the effect on American production and jobs.
