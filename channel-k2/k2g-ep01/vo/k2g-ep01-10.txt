Layer three. The new product: a price on carbon. According to the World Steel Association, making a tonne of steel the classic way — iron from a blast furnace, refined in a basic oxygen furnace — released on average about two point three tonnes of carbon dioxide, based on 2024 data. Melting scrap in an electric arc furnace released about zero point seven. Per tonne of crude steel, less than a third of the emissions. These are the World Steel Association's averages, calculated with its own method; CBAM uses its own rules. Since January 1, 2026, the European Union has put a price on embedded carbon at its border: the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism, or CBAM. As of October 2026, importers bringing more than fifty tonnes of covered goods into the EU in a year, steel among them, must declare the emissions embedded in them and, once a year, surrender certificates to cover those emissions; according to the Commission, certificate sales are scheduled to begin in February 2027. The certificate price follows the EU's carbon market. For goods imported from July to September 2026, the European Commission published it on October 5: eighty-two euros and thirty-two cents per tonne of carbon dioxide. For steel, CBAM counts the direct emissions released in making it, using each plant's reported data or official default values. At the price for July to September 2026, each tonne of carbon dioxide counted adds about eighty-two euros to the certificate bill, before the bill is reduced for free allowances European mills still receive and for any carbon price already paid in the country of production. So how a tonne of steel was made can change what it costs to sell it in Europe. Our read: a tariff sorts steel mainly by where it comes from and what product it is. A carbon price at the border also sorts it by how it was made — plant by plant, where a plant reports its own data.
