Layer one. Near Pittsburgh, the early 1870s. Andrew Carnegie was a Scottish immigrant who had risen through a telegraph office and the Pennsylvania Railroad. In England, he later wrote, he had seen the Bessemer process for making steel demonstrated. Around 1872, Carnegie recalled, he and his partners decided to build. In his autobiography, Carnegie writes that the answer came to him in bed one Sunday morning: a site called Braddock's, "between the Pennsylvania, the Baltimore and Ohio, and the river." Our read: two railroads and a river — three ways in, three ways out. He built there, and named the works after his friend Edgar Thomson, president of the Pennsylvania Railroad. When the Edgar Thomson works began, Carnegie recalled, steel rails sold for about seventy dollars a ton.
