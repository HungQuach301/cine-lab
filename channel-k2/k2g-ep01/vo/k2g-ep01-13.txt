In 1950, America made about eighty-eight million tonnes of steel, almost half the world's. In 2025, about eighty-two million: about four percent. So why guard it like a weapon? Our answer: because steel power was never only about tonnage. It was about share, and about control of the path. Today, more of it runs through other countries' furnaces and other governments' rules. A tariff, a quota and a golden share each set terms, in different ways, for how steel moves across a given border. Whether that is worth what it costs, these numbers can't settle. But the valley at Braddock is still there. The Edgar Thomson works, the mill Carnegie named in the 1870s, was still making steel according to U.S. Steel's annual report to the SEC for 2024, filed in January 2025 — at a company that Japan's Nippon Steel acquired in June 2025, under terms that, as the two companies described them in June 2025, give an American president consent rights over certain decisions.
