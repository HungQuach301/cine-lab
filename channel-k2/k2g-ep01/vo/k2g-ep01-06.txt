You'll often hear that the American steel industry is dying, or already gone. Let's put that next to the record. The U.S. Geological Survey's long series and the World Steel Association's latest figures put American output at about eighty-eight million tonnes in 1950, and about eighty-two million in 2025. By our calculation, a drop of about seven percent. On tonnage, the record says something narrower than "dying." But the line in between was not flat. American production peaked at about one hundred thirty-seven million tonnes in 1973, then fell by half by 1982. The OECD's history describes the worldwide backdrop: optimistic forecasts drew investment into new mills around the world, and then the first oil shock hit. The record we cite doesn't say how much of America's drop that backdrop explains. Today's industry runs differently: in 2025, about seventy-one percent of U.S. steel came from electric arc furnaces, which can run largely on scrap. In China, about eleven percent. That difference matters later, because melting scrap releases far less carbon. Meanwhile, world production rose from one hundred eighty-nine million tonnes in 1950 to more than one point eight billion in 2025 — almost ten times as much. So America's share shrank from almost half to about four percent. By our calculation, most of that change comes from the rest of the world making far more, not from America making less.
