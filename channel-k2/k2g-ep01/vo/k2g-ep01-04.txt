Carnegie's firm then bought into one piece of that path. Carnegie wrote that the supply of superior coke was fixed — "the Connellsville field being defined." In 1882, he recalled, his firm bought half the stock of the Frick Coke Company, run by Henry Frick, and later most of the rest. By 1897, Carnegie wrote, that company held forty-two thousand acres of coal land, more than two-thirds of the Connellsville vein. Our read: this is what's usually called vertical integration — owning steps of the supply chain instead of buying them from outside suppliers. In the same years, there was conflict inside the mills too. In 1892, there was a strike at the Homestead works. A Pennsylvania state historical marker records what happened next: on orders of the Carnegie Steel Company, three hundred Pinkerton agents attempted to land; strikers and citizens repulsed them; seven workers and three Pinkertons were killed. Eight thousand state militia arrived, and by November the strike was broken. Our read: power over the path came alongside power over the people who worked in the mills — and the marker records what that cost at Homestead.
