Meanwhile, look at the other side of the scale. According to the OECD's Steel Outlook 2026, the world's capacity to make steel hit a record in 2025 while demand shrank for a fourth year. The OECD projects the unused part to reach seven hundred forty-five million tonnes by 2028. It notes that many emerging economies have concluded they need a growing national steel industry. When output from new mills can't be absorbed at home, the OECD adds, the steel is typically exported at low prices. Our read: that echoes the old link between steel and national strength. Separately, the OECD reports that Chinese exports of steel products hit a record one hundred thirty-one million tonnes in 2025. By our calculation, and by weight, that's more than all the crude steel the United States produced that year — though exports and crude output are different measures. In a separate finding, the OECD reports that in 2024, subsidies relative to assets, at the median, were about fifteen times higher for Chinese steel firms than for firms in "other partner economies and OECD Member countries," in the OECD's words. The European Union set its own limits. Since the first of July 2026, a new regulation has replaced the old steel safeguard: import quotas of about eighteen point three million tonnes a year, and a fifty percent duty above them. Our read: on both sides of the Atlantic, the question has moved from "who can make steel cheapest?" toward "who is allowed to sell it here at all?"
