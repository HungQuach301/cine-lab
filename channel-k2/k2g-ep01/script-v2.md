[00 HOOK]
In 1950, America made about eighty-eight million tonnes of steel — almost half the world's. In 2025: about eighty-two million. Just four percent. So why is steel now guarded like a weapon?

[01 CONTEXT]
This video explains how the system works. It isn't financial or investment advice.

You are rarely far from steel: the frame of the building around you, the rebar in the road, the body of the car. Our read: a country can import its phones and still function. A country that can't make steel at scale has to buy the skeleton of everything big it builds from someone else.

This story has three layers. The old one: how one river valley in Pennsylvania became the center of American steel. The recent one: how governments, from Washington to Brussels, started putting hard limits on steel imports — in Washington's case, on national security grounds. And the new one: a price now attached to steel crossing one major border — the price of its carbon.

Under all three, our read is that one machine is at work. By the end, you can judge whether we're right.

[02 LAYER1 SITE]
Layer one. Pittsburgh, the early 1870s.

Andrew Carnegie was a Scottish immigrant who had risen through a telegraph office and the Pennsylvania Railroad. In England, he had seen the Bessemer process for making steel demonstrated, and he decided to build.

Railroads would need rails. Where to make them? In his autobiography, Carnegie writes that the answer came to him in bed one Sunday morning: a site called Braddock's, "between the Pennsylvania, the Baltimore and Ohio, and the river." Two railroads and a river. Three ways in, three ways out.

The land cost more than he hoped: ground they expected to buy at five or six hundred dollars an acre, he wrote, cost them two thousand. He built anyway, and named the works after his friend Edgar Thomson, president of the Pennsylvania Railroad. When the Edgar Thomson works began, Carnegie recalled, steel rails sold for about seventy dollars a ton.

[03 LAYER1 CHAIN]
Now look at what Carnegie was actually assembling, because this is the first piece of the machine.

In his autobiography, he wrote down the whole chain behind a single ton of steel. One and a half tons of iron ore: mined, carried by rail about a hundred miles to the Great Lakes, then by boat for hundreds of miles, then by rail again, a hundred and fifty miles, to Pittsburgh. One and a half tons of coal, turned into coke and carried fifty-odd miles. And a ton of limestone, carried a hundred and fifty miles.

Four tons of material, moved hundreds of miles, to make one ton of steel. Years after those first rails, Carnegie wrote that steel was being sold at "three pounds for two cents."

That isn't a secret formula. It's geography plus logistics. Our read: whoever controls the cheapest path from rock to rail gets to set the price everyone else has to meet.

[04 LAYER1 CONTROL]
So Carnegie's firm started securing the path.

Coke came from one field above all. Carnegie wrote that the supply of superior coke was fixed — "the Connellsville field being defined." In 1882, his firm bought half the stock of the Frick Coke Company, run by Henry Frick, and later most of the rest. By 1897, Carnegie wrote, that company held forty-two thousand acres of coal land, more than two-thirds of the Connellsville vein.

Then came mills making, in Carnegie's words, "everything in steel from a wire nail up to a twenty-inch steel girder." The common name for this is vertical integration: own each step, so nobody can charge you for theirs.

At the same time, control reached the mills themselves. In 1892, at the Homestead works, a strike turned into a battle on the riverbank. A Pennsylvania state historical marker records it this way: on orders of the Carnegie Steel Company, three hundred Pinkerton agents attempted to land; strikers and citizens repulsed them; seven workers and three Pinkertons were killed. Eight thousand state militia arrived, and by November the strike was broken.

Our read: owning the path also meant power over the people who worked it, at a cost no price list shows.

[05 LAYER1 POWER]
In March 1901, Carnegie wrote, the banker J. P. Morgan approached him through Charles Schwab and asked whether he really wished to retire from business. Carnegie answered yes. A group led by Morgan and Elbert Gary bought Carnegie's company and combined it with their Federal Steel Company. The result was United States Steel, born in 1901 — the company at the center of layer two.

What did that machine add up to? According to the U.S. Geological Survey, the United States made about nine million tonnes of steel in 1900. In 1944, in the middle of the Second World War, it made about eighty-one million — roughly half of all the steel made on Earth that year. In 1950, still close to half.

That's "steel as national power" in numbers: a share. Our read: part of it came from a world still rebuilding after the war — a peak, not a normal.

[06 MYTH1]
You'll often hear that the American steel industry is dying, or already gone. Let's put that next to the record.

The U.S. Geological Survey's long series and the World Steel Association's latest figures put American output at about eighty-eight million tonnes in 1950, and about eighty-two million in 2025. A drop of about seven percent. Not zero.

But the line in between was not flat. American production peaked at about one hundred thirty-seven million tonnes in 1973, then fell by half by 1982. The industry that came back was built differently: in 2025, about seventy-one percent of U.S. steel came from electric arc furnaces, which can run largely on scrap. In China, about eleven percent. Hold on to those two numbers.

Meanwhile, the world grew. Global production went from one hundred eighty-nine million tonnes in 1950 to more than one point eight billion in 2025 — almost ten times as much. China alone made about nine hundred sixty million. So America's share shrank from almost half to about four percent, mostly because the rest of the world built far more.

[07 LAYER2 TARIFFS]
Layer two. The recent past. Facts as of October 2026.

Governments have formal ways to ask what happens to steel in an emergency. In the United States, one is Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act, a 1962 law that lets the president adjust imports that threaten to impair national security.

In March 2018, a proclamation under that law put a twenty-five percent tariff on imported steel, with Canada and Mexico exempted at first. It said steel imports were "weakening our internal economy," and linked the industry's long-term viability to mills running at about eighty percent of capacity. In February 2025, the tariff was extended to imports from all countries. In June 2025, it was doubled, to fifty percent. In April 2026, the system was rebuilt around that fifty percent, with lower rates for many derivative steel products. A further adjustment in June 2026 is, as of October, the latest.

By its own yardstick? The April 2026 proclamation says that, in the Commerce Secretary's opinion, steel capacity utilization rose from about seventy-two percent in 2017 to about seventy-seven percent — still below the eighty percent mark. That is the administration's own assessment. On the other side, studies of the 2002 U.S. steel tariffs, summarized by the Congressional Research Service, suggested those tariffs hurt the competitiveness and employment of American manufacturers that use steel.

[08 LAYER2 OVERCAPACITY]
Meanwhile, look at the other side of the scale.

According to the OECD's Steel Outlook 2026, world steelmaking capacity hit a record of about two point four billion tonnes in 2025, while demand shrank for a fourth year in a row. The OECD projects the unused part to reach seven hundred forty-five million tonnes by 2028.

When output from new mills can't be absorbed at home, the OECD notes, the steel is typically exported at low prices. It reports that Chinese steel exports hit a record one hundred thirty-one million tonnes in 2025. By weight, that's more than all the crude steel the United States produced that year. The OECD also reports that in 2024, the median Chinese steel firm received about fifteen times as much in subsidies, relative to its assets, as the median producer elsewhere.

The European Union set its own limits. Since the first of July 2026, a new regulation has replaced the old steel safeguard: import quotas of about eighteen point three million tonnes a year, and a fifty percent duty above them.

Our read: on both sides of the Atlantic, the question has moved from "who can make steel cheapest?" toward "who is allowed to sell it here at all?"

[09 LAYER2 GOLDENSHARE]
And then the question reached the company Morgan had built.

In December 2023, U.S. Steel announced it would be acquired by Japan's Nippon Steel. After a national security review, on January 3, 2025, President Biden issued an order prohibiting the acquisition. After a new review, on June 13, 2025, President Trump issued an order allowing it, on the condition of a national security agreement. Five days later, the deal closed.

Under that agreement, as the two companies described it, Nippon Steel committed about eleven billion dollars in new investment by 2028, and the headquarters stays in Pittsburgh. And U.S. Steel issued the U.S. government a "golden share."

A second popular claim worth checking: that the golden share means Washington now owns U.S. Steel. The companies' own announcement describes something narrower. The share lets the government appoint one independent director, and gives the president consent rights over specific decisions: cutting the committed investment, changing the name or headquarters, moving production or jobs out of the country, and certain plant closures or idlings. The full agreement has not been made public.

Three months later, that power came into play without being formally used. U.S. Steel had said it would stop processing steel slabs at its Granite City plant in Illinois. According to the Associated Press on September 22, 2025, the White House said it intervened "with the threat of the president invoking his golden share authority." U.S. Steel reversed course.

Carnegie's firm once secured the path from rock to rail. Today, a government holds consent rights over parts of it.

[10 LAYER3 CARBON]
Layer three. The new product.

The newest cost attached to steel isn't a material. It's carbon.

According to the World Steel Association, making a tonne of steel the classic way — iron from a blast furnace, refined in a basic oxygen furnace — released on average about two point three tonnes of carbon dioxide, based on 2024 data. Melting scrap in an electric arc furnace released about zero point seven. Same product, different route, less than a third of the emissions.

Since January 1, 2026, the European Union has turned that difference into a cost at its border: the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism, or CBAM. As of October 2026, importers bringing more than fifty tonnes of covered goods into the EU in a year, steel among them, must declare the emissions embedded in them, and surrender certificates to cover those emissions. The certificate price follows the EU's own carbon-market auctions. For goods imported from July to September 2026, the European Commission published it on October 5: eighty-two euros and thirty-two cents per tonne of carbon dioxide.

Our own calculation, as an illustration only: at that price, the world-average emissions of the blast-furnace route are worth about one hundred ninety euros per tonne of steel; the scrap route, about fifty-seven. Real declarations use each plant's own emissions or official default values, and the amount due is reduced to reflect free allowances European mills still receive. So no importer pays exactly this. The gap shows the mechanism: how a tonne was made can change what it costs to sell it in Europe.

Remember the furnace numbers: about seventy-one percent electric in the United States, about eleven in China. Our read: a tariff sorts steel mainly by where it comes from. A carbon price at the border sorts it mainly by how it was made — counted plant by plant, not country by country.

[11 LAYER3 HYDROGEN]
Then there's a route that skips coke entirely.

In August 2021, the Swedish steelmaker SSAB announced it had delivered the world's first steel made with iron reduced by fossil-free hydrogen instead of coal and coke: a trial delivery to the Volvo Group, from the HYBRIT partnership with LKAB and Vattenfall.

You'll hear two opposite claims: that green steel is already here, and that it's only marketing. The record sits in between: a trial delivery is real steel, but not yet an industry. A company called Stegra is building a hydrogen-based steel plant in Boden, in northern Sweden. In June 2026 it closed a one point four billion euro financing round, and said its project timeline is under review. As of October 2026, that is the latest public update. We won't guess whether or when it opens.

Our read: a route built on hydrogen depends on new inputs, so where those inputs are may matter again — as coke and ore once mattered in Pittsburgh.

[12 THREAD]
So here is our read of the machine under all three layers.

In the 1880s, power in steel meant controlling the cheapest path from rock to rail: ore, coke, limestone, railroads, river. Carnegie's firm bought or secured many pieces of it.

Since 2018, governments have put hard rules on who may use that path, and Washington treats it as a security question. Tariffs, quotas and a golden share each decide, in different ways, who gets access and on what terms.

A carbon price is a different tool. It doesn't own anything. It's a toll — and a toll changes which paths are cheapest.

Three layers. One question: who controls the path, and who sets the toll?

[13 END]
Back to where we started. In 1950, America made about eighty-eight million tonnes of steel, almost half the world's. In 2025, about eighty-two million: four percent.

So why guard it like a weapon? Our answer: because steel power was never only about tonnage. It was about share, and about control of the path. When America made half the world's steel, Pittsburgh was one of the main stops on that path. Today, more of it runs through other countries' furnaces and other governments' rules. A tariff, a quota and a golden share each work, in different ways, to keep a piece of that path at home.

Whether that is worth what it costs, these numbers alone can't settle. But the valley at Braddock is still there. The Edgar Thomson works, the mill Carnegie named in the 1870s, was still making steel as of U.S. Steel's last annual report, filed in early 2025 — at a company now owned by Japan's Nippon Steel, under an agreement that gives an American president consent rights over certain decisions.
