[00 HOOK]

September 1666. From an alehouse on this bank, a man watches the City burn. “A most horrid malicious bloody flame,” he writes, “not like the fine flame of an ordinary fire.”

This is the City of London, seen from Bankside, across the Thames. Among the towers stands one dome — and much of this skyline is shaped to keep it in view. We are standing near where Samuel Pepys stood on the second night of the Great Fire.

London has burned to the ground twice. Twice, it got the chance to start over. And twice, it rebuilt itself on its old, crooked streets.

Look closely, and the old street pattern is still there, threading between the towers.

To see why, we go back to a Sunday night in September 1666 — to a bakery about a kilometre from here.

[01 FIRE]

Sunday, the second of September 1666. At about one in the morning, fire breaks out in Pudding Lane, in the house of Thomas Farriner, baker to the King.

London is a city of timber. Its houses are packed so tight that their upper storeys almost touch across the lanes.

Nineteen years earlier, the engraver Wenceslaus Hollar had published a long view of this skyline, drawn from St Saviour's church, just along the bank: a forest of church towers, and above them all, the long roof of Old St Paul's.

The summer has been dry, and a fierce east wind is blowing. By morning, the fire has taken the church of St Magnus and most of Fish Street, at the foot of London Bridge.

Samuel Pepys hurries to Whitehall and tells the King that unless houses are pulled down, nothing can stop the fire. The King sends him to the Lord Mayor with that order.

The Lord Mayor, Sir Thomas Bludworth, is overwhelmed. Pepys finds him in Cannon Street, a handkerchief round his neck: "Lord, what can I do? I am spent. People will not obey me." That afternoon, the King and his brother James, Duke of York, take to the river in the royal barge, ordering houses pulled down “apace”.

London Bridge survives. A gap left by an earlier fire, in 1633, stops the flames from crossing to Southwark. Instead, the wind drives the fire west, along the waterfront, and up the hill.

That evening, Pepys takes shelter in "a little ale-house on the Bankside" — about where we are standing. As it grows dark, he sees "one entire arch of fire" — more than a mile long. "It made me weep to see it."

Monday. The fire spreads up Gracechurch Street to Lombard Street, and west to Cannon Street. At two in the afternoon, it takes the Royal Exchange — the City's trading floor.

The roads are jammed with carts.

The river fills with boats — and with goods floating on the water.

John Evelyn walks the burning streets that night. The sky, he writes, is "like the top of a burning oven"; the noise is "like a hideous storm" — the cracking of the flames, the fall of towers, houses and churches.

Tuesday, the fourth. The fire climbs Ludgate Hill to St Paul's Cathedral, wrapped in wooden scaffolding for repairs. Six days before the fire, John Evelyn and Christopher Wren had surveyed its decay — and proposed crowning it with a dome. By evening, the great church is ablaze.

John Evelyn watches its lead run down the streets "in a stream".

In the west, the fire crosses the Fleet and runs down Fleet Street, as far as the Temple. Only now are houses blown up with gunpowder to open firebreaks — first in Tower Street, near the Tower. At the Navy Office, Pepys and a colleague dig a pit in the garden for their wine — and Pepys buries his Parmesan cheese. In the last days of the fire, the King and the Duke work the fire-lines themselves — “even laboring in person,” Evelyn writes.

That night, the wind finally drops. By Thursday, the fire is out.

Homeless Londoners camp in the fields outside the walls — Moorfields, St George's Fields, as far as Highgate — "some under tents, some under miserable huts and hovels."

About thirteen thousand two hundred houses. Eighty-seven parish churches. St Paul's. Roughly four-fifths of the City — over four hundred acres. Fewer than ten deaths were recorded. The real toll is unknown.

[02 REBUILD]

The ashes are still warm when the plans arrive. When Evelyn walks through the ruins, he cannot tell where he is — except by the shell of some church or hall, with a tower still standing.

Within days, Christopher Wren hands the King a new City on paper — straight avenues, open squares, a grid. John Evelyn draws another. Robert Hooke, a third. None of them is built.

Every plot in the burnt City has its owners — and the people who have lost their homes cannot afford to wait years for it all to be redrawn. So Parliament moves faster. From late February 1667, a special Fire Court sits at Clifford's Inn, settling disputes between landlords and tenants. In its first year, it decides three hundred and seventy-four cases.

A Rebuilding Act sets the rules: outside walls of brick or stone, not timber. And a new duty on coal pays for the City's public buildings.

The old street plan stays. Two new streets — King Street and Queen Street — are cut down from the Guildhall toward the river. Rebuilding gets going in 1668. By the end of 1670, about six thousand new houses are up — on the same lanes, in brick.

Near the bakery, Wren and Hooke raise a column: the Monument. Two hundred and two feet high — exactly the distance to the spot in Pudding Lane where the fire began. Portland stone is so hard to come by that the column takes six years.

Fire insurance begins here too. In 1680, Nicholas Barbon opens the Fire Office.

Wren's office rebuilds fifty-one of the City's churches.

And on Ludgate Hill, where Old St Paul's stood, work starts in 1675 on a new cathedral.

The last stone of the lantern is set in 1708 — by Wren's son, Christopher. In 1711, Parliament declares St Paul's complete — three hundred and sixty-five feet, to the top of the cross.

[03 DOME]

For more than two and a half centuries, nothing in London stands taller than the dome.

On London Bridge, the houses and shops are pulled down from 1758. By 1831, a new bridge has replaced the medieval one.

Wren's steeples ring the dome. Even the river moves. Between 1864 and 1870, Joseph Bazalgette's Victoria Embankment pushes the north bank out into the Thames.

In the 1930s, taller office blocks begin to rise near the cathedral, blocking some of its best-known views. But the lanes underneath hardly shift.

In 1937, the cathedral's surveyor, Godfrey Allen, proposes a rule: new buildings must stay low enough to keep the dome in sight from the Thames bridges and the south bank. From 1938, the City applies it. It is called the St Paul's Heights.

[04 BLITZ]

Sunday, the twenty-ninth of December, 1940. In 1666, the fire took four days. This time, the worst of it comes in a single night.

As dusk falls, German bombers begin dropping incendiaries on the City — at least a hundred thousand of them. Around fifteen hundred fires.

It is a Sunday. Many offices are locked and unattended. The water mains are broken, and the Thames is at low tide.

Next to St Paul's, the booksellers' street, Paternoster Row, burns — and with it as many as five million books. Fire crews and volunteers of the St Paul's Watch fight the bombs that land on the cathedral itself. Across the City, the Guildhall's roof burns, and nineteen churches are destroyed.

By morning, the dome is still standing, in a ring of ruins.

About a hundred and sixty people are killed that night — at least twelve of them firefighters.

For the second time in three centuries, a great stretch of the City lies in ruins.

[05 TODAY]

And for the second time, the planners arrive with a clean sheet. In 1946 and 1947, Charles Holden and William Holford draw up a new plan for the City.

Where the bombs hit hardest, it is bold: a new road, London Wall, built in the 1950s; and on the ruins of Cripplegate, from the mid-1960s, the towers of the Barbican.

Everywhere else, it keeps most of the old street lines. The lanes come back.

Slowly, the gaps fill again. And in this century, a cluster of towers climbs to the east, around Bishopsgate and Leadenhall Street.

So when the towers arrive, they rise on the old street pattern — and under the dome's rules. The St Paul's Heights still apply, alongside a set of protected views across London — the farthest from a hill in Richmond Park, ten miles away. The Leadenhall Building tapers as it rises, so that from Fleet Street, the dome still has open sky behind it.

From Bankside tonight, you can stand roughly where Pepys stood, and look across at the same streets. London burned twice. Twice, it could have started over. Twice, it kept its old streets. And the skyline it built still bends around the one building it chose, again and again, to keep in view.
