[00 HOOK]
About one in thirteen American homes sits in a building with two to four apartments. {C01} Of the new homes finished in 2025, about one in a hundred did. {C02} So why did America almost stop building them?

This video explains how the system works. It isn't financial or investment advice. And because the laws and lawsuits here are still moving, everything you'll hear is as of October twelfth, 2026.

[01 CONTEXT]
Picture the classic version on a city corner: a shop on the ground floor, two or three floors of apartments above, one staircase. Most two-to-four-unit buildings are plainer than that — a duplex, a triplex, a fourplex — and just above them are the bigger walk-ups, five to nineteen apartments. Together, they're America's small apartment buildings. Almost a quarter of today's occupied homes in two-to-four-unit buildings were built before 1940. {C03}

Our read of the record: no single cause explains the decline. {C04} What the record does show is three sets of rules that grew up around these buildings — on the map, in the stairwell, and at the bank. Each one has its reasons and its defenders. They don't all hit the same building: the map touches every one of them; the stairwell, mostly the taller walk-ups; the bank, the ones with a shop, or more than four homes. We'll take them one at a time — and then the forces outside the rules.

[02 LOCK1 MAP-ORIGIN]
Lock one: the map.

In September 1921, the U.S. Commerce Department under Herbert Hoover set up an advisory committee on zoning. At that point, Hoover wrote, only forty-eight American cities and towns had zoning ordinances. {C05} The committee drafted a model law for states to copy. Its 1926 edition let cities regulate the height and size of buildings, how much of a lot they could cover, population density, and land use — with different rules in different districts. {C06} Within a year of the act's release, Hoover wrote, eleven states had passed laws modeled wholly or partly on it. {C07}

That same year, a case from Euclid, Ohio — practically a suburb of Cleveland, as the Court put it — reached the Supreme Court. {C55} Euclid had zoned its land in tiers. The strictest allowed single-family houses. The next added two-family homes. The third added apartment houses. Retail stores and shops didn't come in until the fourth. {C08} So a corner shop with apartments above it fit in none of the first three.

In November 1926, the Court upheld the ordinance. {C09} Summing up reports from commissions and experts, Justice George Sutherland wrote that in a district of detached houses, "very often the apartment house is a mere parasite" — and that apartment houses which "in a different environment would be not only entirely unobjectionable but highly desirable" could come close to being nuisances there. {C10} The Court also set a test: if a zoning rule is "fairly debatable," the legislature's judgment controls. {C11} In plain terms, judges would defer to the local lawmakers.

[03 LOCK1 MAP-TODAY]
A century later, how much of the map still works that way?

The National Zoning Atlas, a project that reads zoning codes district by district, counted it for Colorado across 334 jurisdictions. Fifty-nine percent of residential land allows only single-family homes. Just three percent allows buildings of more than four homes "as of right" — meaning without a special approval. {C12}

The map has fine print, too. In Colorado, the Atlas found parking requirements on eighty-five percent of residential land, most often two spaces for every home. {C14} On a small lot, every parking space is room the building can't use.

[04 LOCK2 STAIRS]
Lock two: the stairwell.

Here the rule starts with a safety idea: two separate ways out. Many American building codes start from a model code, the International Building Code — Seattle's, for example, reprints it. {C56} In its 2021 edition, an apartment building may have a single staircase up to the third story, with no more than four homes per floor and sprinklers throughout. A fourth story with one stair? Not permitted. {C15} So a plain duplex or triplex usually isn't affected. The four-, five- and six-story walk-up is.

There's a cost before the stairs, too. In California, a Terner Center study at UC Berkeley noted that one-unit and duplex buildings fall under the residential building code, while projects with three or more homes fall under the stricter commercial code — which it estimated adds roughly ten percent to construction costs. {C57}

On a small lot, a second stair takes a slice of every floor. The Pew Charitable Trusts, in work with the Center for Building in North America — a group that advocates single-stair rules — estimates the extra stairway and corridor take up around seven percent of a building's floor area and add about six to thirteen percent to construction costs. {C16} In a 2024 proposal to Washington's building code council, the state's association of building officials described Seattle's history the same way: on the city's many small lots, "two exits took up a significant portion of the floor plate" {C17} — the usable space on each floor.

Seattle's code has allowed some single-stair apartment buildings since 1977, according to that proposal. Today, it allows them up to six stories, with no more than four homes per floor, and extra conditions such as stronger sprinklers and a pressurized stairway. {C18} New York City allows single-stair apartment buildings of fire-resistant construction up to six stories, if each floor is no more than two thousand square feet. {C19}

[05 LOCK2 SAFETY]
So is one staircase safe enough? The people who study it disagree.

Fire service groups say no. In a March 2024 position statement, the International Association of Fire Chiefs stated its "unwavering support" for keeping a second stairway in apartment buildings over three stories. {C22} California's state fire marshal, in a report to the legislature required by a 2023 law, described "near-unanimous opposition" from California's fire departments to single-stair buildings above three stories, and found that added safeguards "do not fully substitute for the redundancy of two independent stairways." {C23}

The single-stair side points to records. Pew and the Center for Building looked at New York City's 4,440 modern single-stair buildings since 2012, and found the fire-death rate the same as in the city's other residential buildings. {C20} Across New York and Seattle from 2012 to 2024, they counted four fire deaths in these buildings, and say the lack of a second stair played no role in any of them. {C21}

A third study, by engineering consultants for Minnesota's labor department in December 2025, found that almost ninety-seven percent of the building risk it modeled came from sprinklers failing to work. In its analysis, the number of stairs mattered mainly when that failure combined with open doors. {C24}

In a vote announced in May 2026, governmental members of the International Code Council backed a change, eighty-six to thirty-four, that would permit one stair up to four stories in the 2027 model code. {C25}

[06 LOCK3 BANK]
Lock three: the bank.

In American housing finance, one number draws a line: four. A federal regulation from the agency that oversees Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac defines single-family housing as one to four homes, and multifamily as more than four. {C26} Fannie Mae's standard single-family mortgages cover buildings of one to four units, {C27} and so does the FHA's main home-loan program. {C28} So a plain duplex or fourplex can be financed like a house. Five homes or more, and the project moves into multifamily lending.

Now add the shop. Fannie Mae's single-family rules accept a mixed-use property only if it's a one-unit home whose borrower lives there and runs the business. {C29} The FHA allows mixed use in buildings of up to four units, if at least fifty-one percent of the floor area is residential. {C30} So a shop with two apartments above it falls outside Fannie Mae's single-family rule — and a shop with five apartments above it falls outside both.

On the multifamily side, a 2007 Harvard paper quoted a federal commission's housing finance task force calling financing for buildings of five to forty-nine units "one of the most significant gaps in the mortgage industry." {C31} Freddie Mac noted in 2015 that small and large properties default at similar rates — but when small ones do default, losses are higher. {C32} And in 2024, the Terner Center reported that in California, lenders on small projects often require completion guarantees and "recourse to the developer's personal assets" {C33} — the builder's own savings on the line.

[07 OTHER-FORCES]
The rules aren't the whole story. In October 2026, researchers at the Federal Reserve Bank of Minneapolis wrote that high borrowing, operating and construction costs, and low rent growth, "have challenged the profitability of multifamily apartments and slowed multifamily construction in many markets." {C58} The Terner Center modeled small missing-middle buildings in California, and found that in most cases they "would not be built under recent market conditions given costs, rental comps, lack of financing options, and generally unfavorable market conditions." {C59}

How much of the decline comes from the rules, and how much from costs, rates and rents? The record we found doesn't measure that split.

[08 RESULT]
Here's what America built over the same decades.

Two-to-four-unit completions peaked in the 1970s. The steepest fall came in the late 1980s: from about 86,000 homes in 1986 to about 45,000 in 1990. In 2025: 16,000. {C60} The Census numbers show when — not why. As a share of all new homes finished, two-to-four-unit buildings went from almost six percent in the early 1970s to just over one percent in 2025 — again, our calculation. {C36}

The bigger walk-ups fell too: buildings of five to nineteen units went from 377,000 homes completed in 1973 to 48,000 in 2025. {C35} Meanwhile, buildings of fifty or more units went from about one in five new apartments in 1972 to fifty-seven percent in 2025. {C34} New homes moved toward the two ends: houses, and large buildings.

[09 MYTH-RECORD]
So what happens when a place opens the map?

When Washington state's legislature heard its 2023 middle-housing bill, supporters called it "the fastest and most scalable way to increase housing production." Others warned that its "uniform application of requirements does not recognize the uniqueness of each city," and that "more people would just create more traffic." {C61}

Minneapolis offers one test. Its 2040 plan, adopted in 2019, allows duplexes and triplexes in areas once zoned only for single-family homes. {C54} In October 2026, Minneapolis Fed researchers reported that from 2020 through 2025 the city permitted 317 new units in duplexes, triplexes and fourplexes — about two percent of the homes it permitted in multifamily buildings, and roughly the same as a comparison group. {C39} Their summary: changes to land-use rules "appear insufficient to drive new housing production in a challenging development environment." {C40}

California's SB 9 showed a similar pattern in its first year: in 2022, Los Angeles received 211 applications for new units under the law, and San Diego received seven. {C41} In a 2019 study, the planning researcher Yonah Freemark found that upzoning near Chicago rail stations raised property prices but had no measurable effect on newly permitted homes over five years. {C42} On the other side, a 2023 study by Urban Institute researchers linked loosening reforms across U.S. places to a small rise in housing supply — about 0.8 percent within three to nine years, mostly at the higher end of rents. {C43}

So the record sits in between. In the U.S. cases here, small buildings haven't flooded the places that allowed them — and changing the map alone hasn't brought them back, at least not yet.

[10 WHERE-BACK]
So where are these buildings legal again? As of October 12, 2026:

New York City's "City of Yes" plan, approved in December 2024, re-legalized housing above shops on commercial streets in all its low-density districts — two to four stories of homes over a ground-floor business. In the city's own words, these "missing middle" buildings had been "functionally banned by zoning." {C45} Oregon's 2019 law requires cities of 25,000 people or more to allow duplexes through fourplexes, townhouses and cottage clusters. {C46} Washington's 2023 law requires two to six homes per lot, depending on city size and transit. {C47}

The stairwell is moving in some states. Texas lets its cities allow single-stair buildings up to six stories, since September 2025. {C50} Colorado requires cities of 100,000 people or more to allow five stories by December 1, 2027. {C51} And Washington's building code council is targeting October 16th for final adoption of a statewide rule for single-stair buildings up to six stories. {C52}

As of October 2026, the lending line is still four. {C26}

[11 ENDING]
So why did America almost stop building small apartment buildings?

Our read: it isn't that people stopped living in them. About eleven million American homes are in buildings of two to four units, and more than eight in ten of the occupied ones are rented. {C53} Over the same century, three sets of rules settled around these buildings — the map decided where they could stand, the stairwell how tall they could rise, the bank who would lend for them — while costs, rates and rents decided whether the numbers worked at all.

Each rule has defenders, with reasons on the record. In some places, the map and the stairwell have started to change. Whether that brings the building on the corner back is something the record can't tell us yet. Minneapolis's first six years suggest that one change, on its own, isn't enough.
