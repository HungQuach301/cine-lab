[00 HOOK]
About one in thirteen American homes sits in a building with two to four apartments. {C01} Of the new homes finished in 2025, about one in a hundred did. {C02} So why did America stop building them?

[01 CONTEXT]
You know the building. A corner shop on the ground floor. Two or three floors of apartments above. One staircase. A lot of them went up before 1940 — the Census counts almost a quarter of today's occupied two-to-four-unit homes as built before then. {C03}

This video explains how the system works. It isn't financial or investment advice. And because laws and lawsuits here are still moving, everything you'll hear is as of October twelfth, 2026.

The record doesn't hand this decline to a single cause. {C04} What it does show is three sets of rules that grew up around this building — on the map, in the stairwell, and at the bank. Each one, on its own, has a reason. Our read is that, stacked together, they leave very little room for a small building on a corner lot.

[02 LOCK1 MAP-ORIGIN]
Lock one: the map.

In September 1921, the U.S. Commerce Department under Herbert Hoover set up an advisory committee on zoning. At that point, Hoover wrote, only forty-eight American cities and towns had zoning ordinances. {C05} The committee drafted a model law that states could copy — the Standard State Zoning Enabling Act. Its revised 1926 edition let cities regulate the height and size of buildings, the share of a lot they could cover, the density of population, and the use of land — and set different rules in different districts. {C06} Within a year of its first release, Hoover wrote, eleven states had passed laws modeled on it. {C07}

Then a village outside Cleveland went to the Supreme Court. Euclid, Ohio had zoned its land into tiers. In the strictest tier, only single-family houses. Two-family homes came one tier up. Apartment houses one tier above that. And retail stores and shops only in the fourth. {C08} A corner shop with apartments above it fit in neither of the first two tiers.

In November 1926, the Court upheld the ordinance. {C09} Summing up reports from commissions and experts, Justice George Sutherland wrote that, in a neighborhood of detached houses, "very often the apartment house is a mere parasite." {C10} And he set the test courts would lean on afterwards: if a zoning classification is "fairly debatable," the legislature's judgment controls. {C11}

[03 LOCK1 MAP-TODAY]
A century later, how much of the map still works that way?

The best count comes from the National Zoning Atlas, a project that reads zoning codes district by district. In Colorado, across all 334 jurisdictions, it found that most residential land — fifty-nine percent — allows only single-family housing, and just three percent allows more than four units as of right. {C12} In Connecticut, a study by the Urban Institute and Cornell found single-family homes allowed by right on ninety-one percent of land, and multifamily buildings of three or more units on two percent. {C13}

And the map has fine print. In Colorado, the Atlas found parking mandates on eighty-five percent of residential land — most often two spaces for every home. {C14} Every space on a small lot is room the building can't use.

So the first lock decides where this building can go at all. The second decides how tall it can get.

[04 LOCK2 STAIRS]
Lock two: the stairwell.

Most American cities build to a model code, the International Building Code. In its 2021 edition, an apartment building with a single staircase can rise to a third story, with up to four homes per floor and sprinklers throughout. A fourth story? Not permitted with one stair. {C15} Above three floors, you need two.

On a big site, a second stair is a line item. On a small corner lot, it's a slice of every floor. The Pew Charitable Trusts estimates that a second stairway and the corridor joining the two take up around seven percent of a building's floor area, and add roughly six to thirteen percent to construction costs. {C16} In Seattle, building officials described the problem in almost those words: on the city's many small lots, "two exits took up a significant portion of the floor plate." {C17}

That's why Seattle took a different path. Since its 1977 building code, it has allowed some apartment buildings with a single stair, now up to six stories and four homes per floor, with extra conditions — stronger sprinklers and a pressurized stairway among them. {C18} New York City's code also allows single-stair apartment buildings up to six stories, if each floor stays under two thousand square feet. {C19}

[05 LOCK2 SAFETY]
So is one staircase safe? Here the people who study it disagree.

Pew and the Center for Building in North America, a nonprofit that pushes for single-stair rules, looked at New York City's 4,440 modern single-stair buildings since 2012. They found the fire-death rate the same as in the city's other residential buildings. {C20} Across New York and Seattle, from 2012 to 2024, they found four fire deaths in these buildings, and say the missing second stair played no role in any of them. {C21}

Fire leaders read the risk differently. The International Association of Fire Chiefs states its "unwavering support" for keeping a second stairway in apartment buildings over three stories. {C22} California's state fire marshal, in a report to the legislature required by a 2023 law, described "near-unanimous opposition" from the fire service it consulted, and found that added safeguards "do not fully substitute for the redundancy of two independent stairways." {C23} A study for Minnesota's labor department found that almost ninety-seven percent of the building risk it modeled came from sprinklers failing to work — the stair count mattered mainly when that failure combined with open doors. {C24}

As of October 2026, the model code is moving, slowly. In a vote announced this May, the code council's governmental members backed a change, eighty-six to thirty-four, allowing one stair up to four stories. {C25}

[06 LOCK3 BANK]
Lock three: the bank.

In American housing finance, one number draws a line: four. Federal regulation defines single-family housing as one to four homes, and multifamily as more than four. {C26} Fannie Mae's standard single-family mortgages cover buildings of one to four units. {C27} The FHA's main home-loan program does the same. {C28} Five homes or more, and a project moves into multifamily, commercial lending.

Now add the shop. Fannie Mae's single-family rules accept a mixed-use property only if it's a one-unit home that the borrower lives in and whose business they run. {C29} The FHA allows mixed use in buildings of up to four units, if at least fifty-one percent of the floor area is residential. {C30} A shop with six apartments above it fits neither.

On the multifamily side, small buildings have long been the hard case. In a paper for the Millennial Housing Commission, its housing finance task force called financing for buildings of five to forty-nine units "one of the most significant gaps in the mortgage industry." {C31} Freddie Mac noted in 2015 that, when small properties default, losses are higher than for large ones. {C32} And in 2024, the Terner Center at UC Berkeley reported that lenders on small projects often require completion guarantees and recourse to the developer's personal assets. {C33}

[07 RESULT]
Put the three locks together, and look at what America actually builds.

In 1972, buildings of fifty or more units held about one in five new apartments. In 2025, they held fifty-seven percent. {C34} Small apartment buildings — five to nineteen units — went from 377,000 homes completed in 1973 to 48,000 in 2025. {C35} And two-to-four-unit buildings went from almost six percent of all new homes finished in the early 1970s to just over one percent in 2025. That's our calculation from Census completions. {C36}

The middle of the market didn't shrink evenly. Our read: it got squeezed from both ends — single-family houses on one side, large buildings on the other.

[08 MYTH-RECORD]
Here's where the debate usually gets loud. Supporters point to how much housing these reforms make possible: before California's SB 9 passed, a 2021 Terner Center analysis estimated over 700,000 homes would become newly feasible — while cautioning that nowhere near that many were likely to be built. {C37} Opponents warn that statewide mandates "could increase residential density in established neighborhoods" — words from a March 2026 council agenda item in Sterling Heights, Michigan, opposing state zoning bills. {C38} One side hopes a lot will change. The other worries a lot will.

Minneapolis is the longest-running test. Its 2040 plan, adopted in 2019, allows duplexes and triplexes in areas previously zoned only for single-family homes. {C54} This October, researchers at the Federal Reserve Bank of Minneapolis reported that from 2020 through 2025, the city permitted 317 new units in duplexes, triplexes and fourplexes — about two percent of all the homes it permitted in multifamily buildings, and roughly the same as a comparison group. {C39} Their summary: changes to land-use rules "appear insufficient to drive new housing production in a challenging development environment." {C40}

California's SB 9, which lets owners split lots and add homes, tells a similar story so far. In 2022, its first year, Los Angeles received 211 applications for new units under it; San Diego received seven. {C41} And in a study of Chicago, the planning researcher Yonah Freemark found that upzoning near transit raised land prices but produced no measurable increase in new homes over five years. {C42}

Other work points the other way. A national review by Urban Institute researchers linked loosening reforms to a small but statistically significant rise in housing supply — about 0.8 percent within three to nine years, mostly at the higher end of rents. {C43} And when Auckland, New Zealand upzoned about three quarters of its residential land, researchers counted roughly 21,800 extra homes permitted over five years. {C44}

So the record sits in between. Small buildings haven't flooded the places that allowed them — and changing the map alone hasn't brought them back. Our read: that fits the idea of three locks. Opening one leaves two.

[09 WHERE-BACK]
So where can you build one again? As of October 2026, here's where the rules have changed.

New York City's "City of Yes" plan, approved in December 2024, re-legalized housing above shops on commercial streets in its lowest-density districts — two to four stories of homes over a ground-floor business. The city's own description: these "missing middle" buildings had been "functionally banned by zoning." {C45}

Oregon, since 2019, requires its larger cities to allow duplexes through fourplexes and cottage clusters. {C46} Washington state followed in 2023, with two to six homes per lot depending on city size and transit access. {C47} In Montana, the state Supreme Court this March upheld a package of 2023 housing laws, including one requiring cities of more than five thousand people to allow duplexes on home lots, as reported by NBC Montana. {C48} In Arlington, Virginia, a 2023 rule allowing up to six homes per lot is operating while its court case continues; as of this month, the Supreme Court of Virginia is weighing a procedural question in that case. {C49}

The stairwell is moving too. Texas, since September 2025, lets cities allow single-stair buildings up to six stories. {C50} Colorado requires its larger cities to allow five by the end of 2027. {C51} And Washington's building code council is targeting October 16th for final adoption of a statewide rule for single-stair buildings up to six stories. {C52}

The bank line hasn't moved. It's still four. {C26}

[10 ENDING]
So why did America stop building the corner apartment block?

Not because the building stopped working. About eleven million American homes are in buildings of two to four units, and more than eight in ten of the occupied ones are rented. {C53} It's that, over a century, three sets of rules settled on top of it. The map decided where it could stand. The stairwell decided how tall it could rise. And the bank decided who would lend for it.

Each rule has its defenders, with reasons on the record. But for the building on the corner, all three had to say yes at once. As of 2026, in a growing number of places, the first two are starting to.
