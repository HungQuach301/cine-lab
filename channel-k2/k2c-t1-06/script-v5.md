[00 HOOK]
About one in thirteen American homes sits in a building with two to four apartments. {C01} Of the new homes finished in 2025, about one in a hundred did. {C02} So why did America almost stop building them?

Two different Census counts — homes standing today, homes finished in one year — and the shares are our calculation. This video explains how the system works. It isn't financial or investment advice. Facts are as of October twelfth, 2026.

[01 CONTEXT]
Picture the classic version on a city corner: a shop on the ground floor, two or three floors of apartments above, one staircase. The plainer versions are a duplex, a triplex, a fourplex. Just above them sit small apartment buildings of five to nineteen homes. By our calculation from Census survey data, almost a quarter of today's occupied homes in two-to-four-unit buildings went up before 1940. {C03}

Our read: the records we found don't pin the decline on any single cause. {C04} What the record does show is three sets of rules that grew up around these buildings. We'll call them locks — rules that limit what can be built — without judging whether each limit is right. Then we'll look at the forces outside the rules: costs, interest rates and rents.

[02 LOCK1 MAP-ORIGIN]
Lock one: the map.

In 1921, when the Commerce Department under Herbert Hoover set up an advisory committee on zoning, only forty-eight American cities and towns had zoning ordinances, Hoover wrote. {C05} The committee drafted a model law for the powers a state could give its cities — over building height and size, lot coverage, density and land use, with different rules in different districts. {C06} By Hoover's account, eleven states passed laws based on it within a year of its first release. {C07}

In 1926, a case from Euclid, Ohio — "practically a suburb" of Cleveland, the Court said — reached the Supreme Court. {C55} Euclid had zoned its land in tiers: single-family houses only; then two-family homes; then apartment houses. Retail stores and shops didn't come in until the fourth tier. {C08} So a corner shop with apartments above it fit in none of the first three.

The Court upheld the ordinance. {C09} Summing up reports from commissions and experts, Justice Sutherland listed the concerns about apartment houses among detached homes: buildings "interfering by their height and bulk with the free circulation of air and monopolizing the rays of the sun," and bringing traffic and parked cars. Apartment houses which, he wrote, "in a different environment would be not only entirely unobjectionable but highly desirable, come very near to being nuisances" in such districts. {C10} And if a zoning rule was "fairly debatable," the Court said, the legislature's judgment controls {C11} — in plain terms, judges defer to local lawmakers.

[03 LOCK1 MAP-TODAY]
A century later, how much of the map still works that way? Take one state the National Zoning Atlas has mapped. In its 2025 report, covering Colorado's 334 jurisdictions: Fifty-nine percent of residential land allows only single-family homes. Just three percent allows buildings of more than four homes "as of right" — meaning without a special approval. {C12}

The map has fine print, too. In Colorado, the Atlas found parking requirements on eighty-five percent of residential land, most often two spaces for every home. {C14} On a small lot, every parking space is room the building can't use.

[04 LOCK2 STAIRS]
Lock two: the stairwell. The rule starts with a safety idea: two separate ways out.

Many American building codes start from a model, the International Building Code; Seattle's code, for one, is built on it. {C56} Under its 2021 edition, an apartment building may have a single staircase up to the third story, with at most four homes per floor and sprinklers throughout. A fourth story on one stair is not permitted. {C15} So the single-stair limit bites at the fourth story, and at any floor with more than four homes — the taller building on a small lot.

On that lot, a second stair takes a slice of every floor. The Pew Charitable Trusts, working with the Center for Building in North America — a group that advocates single-stair rules — estimates the extra stairway and corridor take around seven percent of the floor area and cost the equivalent of six to thirteen percent of total construction costs. {C16} The Washington Association of Building Officials, explaining Seattle's history, wrote that on its many small lots, "two exits took up a significant portion of the floor plate." {C17}

Seattle has allowed some single-stair apartment buildings since its 1977 code, those officials wrote — under its 2021 code, up to six stories, with at most four homes per floor and extra fire protections, including a pressurized stairway. {C18} New York City's 2022 code allows them up to six stories for its Type I and Type II construction — the two noncombustible types — if each floor is no more than two thousand square feet. {C19}

There's a code cost below four stories, too. A 2024 Terner Center study at UC Berkeley noted that in California, buildings with three or more homes fall under the stricter commercial building code — adding, by its estimate, roughly ten percent to construction costs. {C57}

[05 LOCK2 SAFETY]
So is one staircase safe enough? Fire officials, researchers and code bodies disagree.

In a March 2024 position statement, the International Association of Fire Chiefs declared its "unwavering support" for keeping a second stairway in apartment buildings over three stories. {C22} In an undated joint statement, the International Association of Fire Fighters and the Metropolitan Fire Chiefs Association wrote: "Put simply, lives will be endangered." {C62} California's state fire marshal, in a report required by a 2023 law, described "near-unanimous opposition" from California's fire departments to single-stair buildings above three stories, and found added safeguards "do not fully substitute for the redundancy of two independent stairways." {C23}

Pew and the Center for Building, the advocacy group, point to records: in New York City's 4,440 modern single-stair buildings since 2012, they found the fire-death rate the same as in the city's other residential buildings, {C20} and they say a missing second stair played no role in the four deaths they counted in New York and Seattle's modern single-stair buildings from 2012 to 2024. {C21}

A modeling study by engineering consultants for Minnesota's labor department, in December 2025, found that, in its risk model, the number of stairs made a difference only when the sprinklers failed and both the apartment door and the stair door on the fire floor were open. {C24}

In a vote announced in May 2026, governmental members of the International Code Council backed a change, eighty-six to thirty-four, to allow one stair up to four stories in the 2027 model code — which, as of October 12th, has not been published. {C25}

[06 LOCK3 BANK]
Lock three: the bank.

In housing finance, one number draws a line: four. The Federal Housing Finance Agency defines, in its housing-goals regulation, single-family housing as one to four homes, and multifamily as more than four. {C26} Fannie Mae's and the FHA's single-family loan programs cover buildings of one to four units. {C27} {C28} So a plain fourplex falls under the same single-family loan programs as a house. Add a fifth home, and the project moves into multifamily lending.

Now add the shop. Fannie Mae's single-family rules accept a mixed-use property only if it's a one-unit home whose borrower lives there and runs the business. {C29} The FHA allows mixed use up to four units, if at least fifty-one percent of the floor area is residential. {C30} So a shop with five apartments above it fits neither single-family program.

On the multifamily side, small buildings have their own problems. An older assessment — a 2007 Harvard paper quoting a federal commission's finance task force — called financing for buildings of five to forty-nine units "one of the most significant gaps in the mortgage industry." {C31} Freddie Mac noted in 2015 that small and large properties default at similar rates, but losses are higher when small ones do. {C32} And the same Terner Center study reported that in California, lenders on small projects often require completion guarantees and "recourse to the developer's personal assets." {C33}

[07 OTHER-FORCES]
The rules aren't the whole story. In October 2026, researchers at the Federal Reserve Bank of Minneapolis wrote that high borrowing, operating and construction costs, and low rent growth, "have challenged the profitability of multifamily apartments and slowed multifamily construction in many markets." {C58} And in 2024, the Terner Center at UC Berkeley found that its small model buildings in California, in most cases, "would not be built under recent market conditions." {C59}

So, to recap: the map can block any of these buildings. The stairwell bites mainly at four stories and up. The bank line bites at five homes, and for many buildings with a shop. And then come costs, rates and rents. How much of the decline comes from each? The record we found doesn't measure that split.

[08 RESULT]
Here's what America built over the same decades.

Two-to-four-unit completions peaked in the 1970s, then fell in waves: from about 86,000 homes in 1986 to 45,000 in 1990, and from about 41,000 in 2006 to 9,000 in 2010. In 2025: 16,000. {C60} The Census numbers show when — not why; this video doesn't test what caused each drop. As a share of all new homes, two-to-four-unit buildings went from almost six percent in the early 1970s to just over one percent in 2025 — our calculation from Census completions, pooling 1970 through 1974. {C36}

Small apartment buildings of five to nineteen units went from 377,000 homes completed in 1973, the series' peak year, to about 48,000 in 2025 — our sum of two Census columns. {C35} Single-family houses went from about fifty-seven percent of new homes in the early 1970s to about sixty-eight percent in 2025 — again our calculation, pooling 1970 through 1974. {C63} And in a separate Census series, buildings of fifty or more units went from one in five new apartments in 1972 to fifty-seven percent in 2025. {C34} New homes moved toward the two ends: houses, and large buildings.

[09 MYTH-RECORD]
So what happens when a place opens the map?

When Washington state's legislature heard its 2023 middle-housing bill, supporters called it "the fastest and most scalable way to increase housing production." In the committee's staff summary of the testimony, others said its "uniform application of requirements does not recognize the uniqueness of each city," and that "more people would just create more traffic." {C61}

Minneapolis offers one test. Its 2040 plan, adopted in 2019, allows — as of October 2026 — duplexes and triplexes in areas once zoned only for single-family homes. {C54} In October 2026, Minneapolis Fed researchers reported that from 2020 through 2025 the city permitted 317 new units in duplexes, triplexes and fourplexes — about two percent of the homes it permitted in multifamily buildings, and roughly the same as a comparison group. {C39} Their headline also reported stable rents; this video doesn't test what caused them. Their summary: land-use changes "appear insufficient to drive new housing production in a challenging development environment." {C40}

California's 2021 law SB 9 lets homeowners split their lot and build up to four homes on a single-family parcel. In its first year, 2022, Los Angeles received 211 applications for new units under the law; San Diego received seven. {C41} In a 2019 study, the researcher Yonah Freemark found that upzoning near Chicago rail stations raised property prices but had no measurable effect on newly permitted homes over five years. {C42}

Other studies find gains. A 2023 study by Urban Institute researchers linked loosening reforms across U.S. places to a rise in housing supply of about 0.8 percent within three to nine years, mostly at the higher end of rents. {C43} And outside the U.S., a 2023 University of Auckland working paper estimated that roughly 21,800 additional homes were permitted in the five years after Auckland upzoned about three quarters of its residential land — about four percent of its housing stock. {C44}

So the record sits in between. In Minneapolis through 2025, and in Los Angeles and San Diego in SB 9's first year, allowing more homes per lot didn't produce a surge of small buildings. Freemark's Chicago study found no measurable effect on permits; the Urban Institute and Auckland studies found gains. These studies measure different things in different places, so they don't add up to one number — and none of them measures the traffic or local-character concerns raised in Washington.

[10 WHERE-BACK]
So where are these buildings legal again? As of October 12, 2026:

New York City's "City of Yes" plan, approved by the City Council on December 5, 2024, re-legalized housing above shops on commercial streets in all its low-density districts — two to four stories of homes over a ground-floor business. In the Department of City Planning's final plan, these "missing middle" buildings had been "functionally banned by zoning." {C45} Oregon's 2019 law requires cities of 25,000 people or more to allow duplexes through fourplexes, townhouses and cottage clusters in areas zoned for detached single-family homes. {C46} Washington's 2023 law requires many cities to allow at least two to six homes per lot, depending on city size and distance to major transit. {C47}

On stairs: Texas lets its cities allow single-stair buildings up to six stories, with at most four homes per floor, since September 2025. {C50} Colorado requires cities of 100,000 people or more to allow five-story single-stair apartment buildings by December 1, 2027. {C51}

And the lending line is still four. {C26}

[11 ENDING]
So why did America almost stop building them?

Our read: no single lock did it. The buildings themselves didn't go away — about eleven million American homes are in buildings of two to four units, and more than eight in ten of the occupied ones are rented. {C53} But three sets of rules shape new ones: the map decides where they can stand, the stairwell how tall the small apartment buildings above them can rise, the bank which loan programs they fit. And costs, rates and rents decide whether the numbers work at all. The record we found doesn't measure how much each one contributes.

Each of those rules has defenders, with reasons on the record. In some places, the map and the stairwell have started to change. Whether that brings back the building on the corner, the record doesn't settle yet.
